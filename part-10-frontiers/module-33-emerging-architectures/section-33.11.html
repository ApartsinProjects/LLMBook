33.11 What 2026 Settled (and What Remains Open)

Ten debates the field resolved in 2026, and ten that remain genuinely open.

"The most dangerous thing about a new technology is not what it changes; it is how confidently people predict what won't change."

Scale, Computationally Devout AI Agent
Why this page exists

The field has been arguing with itself for years. Some of those arguments resolve; some don't. This page marks which arguments actually settled in 2026, with concrete evidence, and which ones are still genuinely open. We hope it ages well; we expect parts of it won't.

Ten Debates 2026 Settled

1. Scaling laws still hold, with caveats

The 2024 debate: Has pretraining hit a wall? Are we out of data? Have scaling laws stopped working?

2026 verdict: Scaling laws still hold for pretraining loss, but loss is no longer the right north star. The frontier moved to compute spent on post-training and inference (reasoning models). Pretraining gains continued but saturated relative to the cost; the marginal dollar moved to RLVR, GRPO, and inference-time search. Chinchilla-optimal is the right starting point; what you do after is where the slope is steepest.

Evidence: GPT-5, Claude 4 Opus, Gemini 3 all spent more on post-training than on pretraining (publicly stated for Claude 4; inferred from compute disclosures for the others).

2. Open-weight models caught up, for most tasks

The 2025 debate: Are open-weight models permanently 6–12 months behind frontier closed models?

2026 verdict: The 6-month gap closed on most tasks. DeepSeek-R2, Llama 4, Qwen 3, and Mistral Large 3 all landed within a few percentage points of contemporaneous closed frontier on standard benchmarks. The gap that remains is on the hardest reasoning benchmarks (FrontierMath, ARC-AGI-2, Codeforces) where the closed labs still lead by a generation. For 95% of production use-cases, open-weight is now competitive on quality and dominant on cost.

Implication: "What model do I use?" is increasingly a deployment-model question, not a quality question. (See Section 31.4: Build vs. Buy.)

3. Long context does not eliminate RAG

The 2024 debate: With 1M-token (then 2M, then 10M) context windows, is RAG obsolete?

2026 verdict: RAG is alive and the use-cases are clearer than before. Long context dominates one-off analysis where you want every page in scope. RAG dominates anywhere you have more than a few documents, need fresh content, care about cost-per-query, or want auditability of which sources contributed. "Lost in the middle" turned out to be more durable than predicted; long-context attention quality is still measurably worse than retrieval over chunks for multi-hop questions.

Where the two converged: Long-context-aware retrieval pipelines (e.g., contextual chunking, parent-doc retrieval expanded to a 200K window) became the dominant pattern. Pure long-context-no-retrieval lost.

4. Reasoning models are a real paradigm, not a fad

The 2024 debate: Is o1 a one-off trick or a new paradigm?

2026 verdict: Reasoning is a new compute axis. o3 and Claude 4 Opus reasoning mode reliably solve problems that no amount of zero-shot prompting at any scale ever cracked, FrontierMath, IMO problems, complex multi-step coding. Crucially, the open-weight world reproduced the recipe (DeepSeek-R1, then R2; Qwen QwQ, then Qwen3-Reasoner) within months, confirming this wasn't a closed-lab moat.

What's still being figured out: When to spend the extra tokens. For trivial tasks reasoning models burn money. The "router that picks reasoning vs. fast model per query" became a standard 2026 production pattern.

5. Most "multi-agent systems" were one agent in a wig

The 2024 debate: Are multi-agent architectures the next breakthrough, or hype?

2026 verdict: Most multi-agent demos work because the underlying model is good enough that any prompting structure looks intelligent. Controlled studies (e.g., the Princeton multi-agent ablations) consistently found that "one good agent given more reasoning budget" matched or beat the multi-agent equivalent at the same total token cost. Exception: tasks with naturally parallelizable subtasks (research, broad search, multi-source synthesis), where genuine speedups and modest quality wins emerged.

Practical guidance: Default to single-agent. Add agents only when you have a parallelizable workload or a specific control reason (e.g., one agent for planning, one for execution, with logged hand-off).

6. MCP won the tool-protocol war

The 2024-25 debate: How will agents and tools talk to each other? OpenAI function calling? Anthropic's MCP? Google's something? LangChain's anything?

2026 verdict: The Model Context Protocol (MCP) became the de-facto standard for exposing tools to agents. All major labs adopted it; the open-source ecosystem coalesced around it; framework lock-in started receding because moving an agent between providers stopped meaning rewriting tool definitions. A2A protocols for agent-to-agent communication are still consolidating; expect MCP-A2A or a successor to settle in 2027.

Where to read more: Chapter 22 covers MCP in detail and contrasts it with provider-specific function-calling JSON.

7. SWE-bench saturation is real, but doesn't generalize

The 2024-25 debate: When will coding agents hit 90%+ on SWE-bench Verified?

2026 verdict: Frontier agents reliably hit 70–85% on SWE-bench Verified. But SWE-bench is a curated set of resolvable issues; the broader software-engineering distribution is much harder. Real-world agentic coding (running in production at vendors and inside large engineering teams) saw resolution rates closer to 30–50% on internal benchmarks where the issue might not be resolvable at all, or might require cross-system context the agent doesn't have. SWE-bench is a great training target and a misleading capability claim.

What replaced it: A new generation of benchmarks (SWE-bench Multi-step, Devin Eval, the unnamed-but-rumored "real engineering" benchmarks from Anthropic and OpenAI) emerged in late 2026 to fill the gap. Expect those to be the headline metric in 2027.

8. Hallucination got measurably better, not solved

The 2024 debate: Will hallucination be solved by scale alone, by better training, by RAG, or by some combination?

2026 verdict: Across-the-board measurable improvement on standard hallucination benchmarks (FaithBench, TruthfulQA, HaluEval) of 30–60% from 2023–2024 baselines. But the absolute rate is still non-zero; for any production system with non-trivial stakes you still need verifier loops or human-in-the-loop. The current best practice is the generator-verifier pattern: a fast model produces, a slower (or constrained, or grounded) model verifies, and the user only sees verified output.

Cross-reference: Section 19.1 for the mechanism; Section 30.2 for the safety frame.

9. Eval moved from "afterthought" to "the actual job"

The 2024 debate: Will teams ever take LLM eval seriously?

2026 verdict: Yes, mostly because the alternative stopped working. As LLM products matured, the teams that won were the ones with the strongest eval discipline; the teams that shipped without eval got blindsided by regressions, agent drift, and silent prompt-injection compromises. LLM-as-judge with periodic human calibration became the default. Eval-driven quality gates (the test you must pass before a prompt change ships) became standard infrastructure.

Open question: How to evaluate agents at task-level, not just step-level. Still unresolved.

10. The EU AI Act became real

The 2024-25 debate: Will the EU AI Act actually be enforced, or quietly diluted?

2026 verdict: Enforcement began in earnest in mid-2026. Several high-profile cases (a fintech using LLM credit scoring; a hospital using LLM triage; a recruitment platform using LLM screening) resulted in actual fines and operational changes. The classification framework (unacceptable / high-risk / limited-risk / minimal-risk) is now a fixed cost of doing AI business in Europe, and increasingly the de-facto standard exported to other jurisdictions.

Practical guidance: If your system touches EU users in any capacity, you should know your classification. Section 30.9 walks you through it.

Ten Debates Still Open at the End of 2026

Some questions everyone wishes 2026 had answered, but it didn't. These will likely dominate 2027:

  1. Where does the next 10x in raw capability come from? Pretraining slope is flattening relative to dollars spent. Reasoning compute scales well but saturates per-problem. Multi-agent and tool use add capability but not generality. The actively contested theories: world models, neuro-symbolic hybrids, continual learning architectures, embodied training, larger context with better attention quality. Pick a hypothesis; choose a thesis topic.
  2. Will alternative architectures (Mamba, RWKV, hybrid state-space) ever displace transformers? 2026 saw production deployments of state-space models at the edge (latency wins). Frontier labs remain transformer-only. The crossover, if it happens, is still ahead.
  3. What does "agency" mean operationally? The field disagrees on whether agents are categorically different from sequential tool use, or just a name for a pattern. The legal, safety, and product implications of this disagreement remain unresolved.
  4. How do you align a system you cannot fully evaluate? RLHF + DPO assume you can rank outputs. As outputs become superhuman (on hard math, on novel code, on multi-step plans), human ranking becomes unreliable. Scalable oversight is the technical name; nobody has it solved.
  5. Will training data exhaustion be a real problem? Synthetic-data pipelines have absorbed most of the alarm, but the long-tail distribution shifts subtly with each generation. The collapse-vs-survival debate (Shumailov et al. revisited) is still unresolved at scale.
  6. Who pays for the compute? Centralized inference (a few hyperscalers) vs. distributed inference (laptops, phones, on-prem servers) vs. specialized accelerators. Economics, policy, and physics all push different directions. 2026 didn't pick.
  7. Does interpretability scale? Sparse autoencoders work for small models. The frontier ones remain mostly opaque. Whether SAE-style techniques generalize to 1T-parameter MoEs is one of the most important open research questions.
  8. What is the right unit of evaluation for production AI? Per-turn? Per-task? Per-user-session? Per-business-outcome? The lack of consensus makes apples-to-apples vendor comparison nearly impossible.
  9. Will robotics and embodied AI actually arrive? Vision-language-action models showed real progress in 2026 (Pi-0.5, RT-2 successors). The gap between lab demos and reliable household robots is closing but still measured in years, not months.
  10. Will the field reach a regulatory equilibrium or a regulatory winter? The EU is concrete; the US is fragmented; China is opaque-but-active. Whether 2027 is the year of harmonization or the year of fragmentation is the single most consequential open question for the industry.
How to use this list

If you're a researcher: every item in "still open" is a candidate research direction. The ones with the most leverage are #4 (scalable oversight), #7 (interpretability at scale), and #1 (next-10x-capability).

If you're a founder: every item in "settled" should be in your strategy doc. Items in "still open" are sources of strategic risk; bet on the assumption you most believe in and explicitly hedge the others.

If you're a student: the gap between this list and what your textbook published last year tells you how fast you have to read. Use the 2026 Freshness Index to keep up.
