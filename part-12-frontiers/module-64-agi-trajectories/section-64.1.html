Frontier Benchmarks: HLE, ARC-AGI-2, FrontierMath

Section 64.1

For most of the LLM era, "the benchmark" was an evolving but mostly tractable target: GLUE, then SuperGLUE, then MMLU, then GSM8K, then BIG-Bench, then MMLU-Pro, then SWE-bench. Each held the field's attention for roughly a year before being saturated by a frontier model and superseded by something harder. The benchmarks of 2025-2026 are different. Humanity's Last Exam, ARC-AGI-2, and FrontierMath are not designed to be saturated within a year; they are designed to remain unsaturated by the strongest models the field can produce today, and possibly for several years to come.

This section walks through the three current frontier benchmarks, the scores the strongest models have achieved on them as of mid-2026, and what those scores tell us about the gap between current systems and the more ambitious claims about AGI. The benchmarks are the empirical floor under the timeline debate that occupies Section 64.3; whatever you believe about 2027 or 2033, the benchmark numbers are the constraint your beliefs have to fit.

Why Hard Benchmarks Are The Right Floor

Benchmarks that saturate quickly tell you about model improvement on a narrow capability slice. Benchmarks designed to resist saturation for years tell you about the trajectory toward general human-expert-level capability. The three discussed here, HLE, ARC-AGI-2, and FrontierMath, are explicitly designed to require capabilities the field believes are beyond the current generation. Tracking them is therefore the closest available proxy for tracking the gap between deployed systems and the AGI-class capability that timeline forecasters argue about.

Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)

Humanity's Last Exam, introduced by Phan et al. in January 2025, is a 2,500-question multimodal benchmark spanning the limits of human expert knowledge across mathematics, science, humanities, and professional domains. The questions are written by domain experts (PhD-level scholars in their fields) and curated specifically to be questions that current frontier LLMs answer wrong while domain experts answer right. The benchmark is multimodal: many questions include diagrams, plots, or scientific imagery the model has to interpret.

As of mid-2026, the leading scores on HLE are striking precisely because they are not near saturation. The HLE leaderboard and Artificial Analysis's tracking report Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview at 44.7%, GPT-5.5 (xhigh reasoning) at 44.3%, and GPT-5.5 (high reasoning) at 43.0%. Roughly half of HLE remains unsolved by the strongest model in the world. The benchmark designers explicitly anticipated this; they wanted a benchmark where 90%+ would represent a meaningful step beyond current frontier capability rather than something the next quarterly release would saturate.

Key Insight: Reasoning Budget Helps But Doesn't Solve

The gap between GPT-5.5 (xhigh reasoning) at 44.3% and GPT-5.5 (high reasoning) at 43.0% on HLE is small, ~1.3 percentage points, despite "xhigh" using substantially more inference compute per question. Spending more test-time tokens helps, but not nearly enough to close the 55% gap to a hypothetical 100% score. This is one of the cleanest empirical signals that the frontier-reasoning paradigm has diminishing returns on the hardest questions. Doubling reasoning compute does not double accuracy on HLE; it adds a percentage point or two. Whether this represents an algorithmic ceiling on the current reasoning approach or a temporary plateau is one of the open questions of 2026.

ARC-AGI-2 and the Reasoning Challenge

François Chollet's ARC-AGI-2 (May 2025) is the second-generation Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus, redesigned after the original ARC was largely conquered by 2024-25 frontier models. ARC-AGI was built around the premise that "intelligence is the efficiency with which a system acquires new skills" rather than the breadth of skills it has already acquired. The benchmark consists of grid-based puzzles where the rule is given by example: two or three input-output pairs, and the system must infer the underlying transformation and apply it to a fresh input.

The headline 2025 result for ARC-AGI-2: the top Kaggle submission achieved 24% accuracy at a cost of approximately $0.20 per task. The benchmark designers consider this a meaningful but small step; the goal is approximately 85% accuracy at low cost, which they argue would represent a qualitative change in machine reasoning. The early 2026 ARC-AGI-3 technical report describes a planned interactive-reasoning successor that introduces multi-step environments, on the premise that even ARC-AGI-2 underestimates the difficulty of "novel reasoning in unfamiliar contexts."

The ARC-AGI line is the benchmark closest in spirit to "AGI as a capability." A system that scores 85%+ on ARC-AGI-2 cheaply has demonstrated something the rest of the LLM literature has not: efficient adaptation to genuinely novel structure. Whether that capability is necessary or sufficient for AGI is a deep philosophical question, but it is one of the most defensible operationalizations the benchmark community has produced.

FrontierMath

FrontierMath, from Glazer et al. at Epoch AI, is a benchmark of mathematical research-level problems written by professional mathematicians. The problems are not contest math; they are problems analogous to those a working mathematician would tackle in research, requiring deep domain knowledge and multi-step formal reasoning. Each problem has a single numeric or simple-string answer, so grading is unambiguous, but solving requires the kind of reasoning that previously required hours of human expert effort.

The leaderboard structure is tiered. Tiers 1-3 cover progressively harder undergraduate-through-early-research-level problems; Tier 4, added in June 2025, contains 50 problems that the benchmark designers describe as at the level of an active research mathematician. The best single-run score on Tier 4 as of mid-2026 is 29%; the "ever-solved" rate on Tiers 1-3 reaches above 40% with the strongest models (GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.6) using high reasoning compute. These are not saturation-class numbers, but they are remarkable in absolute terms: a measurable fraction of research-mathematician-level problems are now solvable by frontier models within minutes.

Practical Example: What 29% On FrontierMath Tier 4 Means

The Tier 4 problems are vetted by mathematicians who estimate that a human research mathematician working in the relevant subfield would take hours to days per problem. A 29% accuracy from a frontier model that spends roughly 10-30 minutes of inference compute per problem is a productivity-multiplier-class result, not a saturation result. The same model on undergraduate-level math benchmarks scores in the 90s. The Tier 4 number tells you that "expert-level mathematical reasoning" is now a partial capability of frontier models, available about a third of the time. That is the practical sense in which we are at a serious capability frontier and not yet at AGI.

Why These Three, And Why Not Saturating

The pattern across HLE, ARC-AGI-2, and FrontierMath is that all three are explicitly designed to require capabilities the field believes are beyond the 2026 frontier, and all three are returning sub-50% scores from the strongest available models. The 2024-style "the benchmark will saturate in a year" pattern has not held for these three. Two questions follow:

First, what is bottlenecking the scores? The cleanest answer is "post-training reasoning compute does not yet scale to the difficulty of these problems." More inference compute helps, but with sharply diminishing returns visible in the HLE comparison above. The architectural and post-training innovations that produced reasoning models bought roughly 20 percentage points on these benchmarks between 2024 and 2026; whether the next 20 are available within the same paradigm is an open question.

Second, do these benchmarks measure something useful for AGI forecasting? The community is divided. Optimistic readings: HLE, ARC-AGI-2, and FrontierMath collectively cover (a) the breadth of human expert knowledge, (b) efficient reasoning under novelty, and (c) deep domain-specific multi-step reasoning. A system that scored 90%+ on all three would have crossed something the field would have to take seriously as an AGI-class capability. Pessimistic readings: even saturating these benchmarks would leave open whether the system can act over long horizons, learn continually, or operate effectively in physical or social environments, all of which AGI-class capability presumably requires.

Caveat: Benchmark Scores Are Necessary, Not Sufficient

Every named benchmark above has at least one critique in the literature arguing that the benchmark fails to test some capability AGI would require. HLE is multiple-choice-tractable on many questions; ARC-AGI-2 is grid-based and may not transfer to language; FrontierMath has a single numeric answer per problem, so it tests answer-finding more than proof construction. Any single-benchmark claim about AGI is therefore weak. The strongest claim defensible from current data is that a frontier model is now a competent partial collaborator for an expert in any of these domains, not that it has replaced the expert.

What's next: hard capability evaluations on benchmarks like HLE are one constraint on the AGI timeline debate. The other is the alignment question: even if a model could solve HLE, would we want it deployed, and could we trust it at the scales involved? The next section turns to alignment at frontier scale.
