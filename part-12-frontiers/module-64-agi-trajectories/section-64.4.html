Economic Implications & Labor-Market Data

Section 64.4

For most of LLM history, the economic conversation about AI happened in two registers: optimistic projections about productivity gains and apocalyptic projections about mass displacement. Both were largely speculative. By 2026, neither register is appropriate; we have actual labor-market data on deployed AI, and the picture is more nuanced than either extreme. Adoption is high (35.9% of U.S. workers used generative AI by December 2025) and growing fast. Job displacement directly attributable to AI is non-zero but smaller than the dramatic projections (roughly 55,000 of 1.17 million layoffs in 2025, about 5%). Productivity effects are real but heterogeneous, with some occupations seeing substantial augmentation and others almost none.

This section walks through the four datasets that define the empirical economic picture in 2026: Anthropic's labor-market data drawn from observed Claude usage, the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, the BLS occupation-level employment data, and the 2026 "AI Skills Shift" academic literature. The headline finding is that the augmentation-versus-automation question, which the field has been arguing about in the abstract for years, now has empirical answers, and they are largely favorable to the augmentation reading.

The Augmentation-vs-Automation Question, Finally Empirical

The classic labor-economics question for AI: do these systems augment the human worker (making the worker more productive) or automate the worker (making the worker unnecessary)? In 2024 this was a theoretical question; in 2026 it has data behind it. Anthropic's measurement of its own deployed system shows 78.7% of measured Claude interactions are augmentation pattern, 21.3% are automation pattern. That ratio is not destiny, but it is the strongest empirical signal available about what AI is actually doing in production, not what it could in principle do.

The Anthropic Labor-Market Project

Anthropic's labor-market impacts research is the most cited 2025-2026 dataset on deployed AI economic effects. The methodology: classify a representative sample of Claude conversations by occupation (using the U.S. O*NET taxonomy) and by interaction pattern (augmentation versus automation). The 2025 release reported that 78.7% of Claude interactions fall into augmentation patterns (the user is doing work and getting help) and 21.3% into automation patterns (the user is delegating a task to be completed). The split varies substantially by occupation: software engineering and writing skew more automation-leaning, healthcare and education skew more augmentation-leaning.

The deployment-level numbers are equally striking. By December 2025, 35.9% of U.S. workers reported using generative AI in their job in the previous month, up from roughly 25% a year earlier. Adoption is not uniform: knowledge workers in tech, marketing, and analytics sit at 60%+ usage; manual-labor occupations sit below 10%. The strongest single predictor of usage is "how much of your work happens on a keyboard," which is roughly what the labor literature would have predicted.

Key Insight: Augmentation Dominates In Production

The 78.7% augmentation rate in Anthropic's data is the single most informative number for thinking about AI's labor impact through 2026. If automation were the dominant pattern in deployment, we would expect (a) much sharper layoff patterns concentrated in the most AI-exposed occupations, (b) clear productivity-level changes correlated with AI adoption, and (c) wage divergence between high-AI-exposure and low-AI-exposure roles. None of these have shown up cleanly in the 2025 data. The pattern that has shown up is high adoption among workers who use AI as a coworker, not a replacement. This is the empirical floor under the "augmentation" reading.

Layoff Data: Smaller Than The Hype, Real Nonetheless

The 2025 U.S. layoff data is the strongest case for the "AI is causing significant displacement" reading, but it is more modest than headlines would suggest. Total announced layoffs in the U.S. for 2025 were approximately 1.17 million, per Challenger, Gray & Christmas's monthly job-cuts report. Of those, roughly 55,000 (about 4.7%) were attributed by employers specifically to AI substitution. This is a non-trivial number in absolute terms, especially concentrated as it was in specific industries (customer service, junior software roles, content production), but it is far smaller than the "tens of millions of jobs displaced" projections that circulated in 2023-2024.

Two confounders matter. First, attribution is unreliable: a layoff in a department where AI was recently adopted may or may not have been "caused" by AI in any meaningful sense, and corporate communications skew the attribution in different directions for different audiences. Second, the displacement effect is forward-loaded relative to its visibility: roles that would have been hired but were not (because AI substituted for the hire) do not show up in layoff statistics at all. The honest summary is that the 2025 layoff data is consistent with a moderate AI displacement effect but does not by itself establish a large one.

The WEF Projection

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects that by 2030, AI and related technologies will displace approximately 92 million jobs globally and create approximately 170 million, for a net positive of about 78 million. The methodology aggregates employer surveys with occupational-task analyses; it is not a forecast of automation rate but of net employment effects.

The 92M-displaced number is consistent with the augmentation-dominant reading from Anthropic's data, in the sense that displacement is real and material but is smaller than gross job churn. The 170M-created number is more speculative, because it depends on industries that do not yet exist in 2026 being substantial employers by 2030 (AI-tooling roles, AI-augmented professional services, robotics and embodied-AI infrastructure). The net-positive headline is conditional on these new categories materializing.

The Skills Shift Literature

The April 2026 paper "The AI Skills Shift: Mapping Skill Obsolescence, Emergence, and Transition Pathways in the LLM Era" is the canonical academic reference for what skills are appreciating and depreciating with AI deployment. The headline findings: skills that involve generating draft content, summarizing documents, and writing routine code have depreciated substantially (these are now things any worker can do with a prompt). Skills that involve verification, judgment, taste, and end-to-end project management have appreciated. The pattern is consistent across white-collar occupations.

This is the "complementarity" reading of skill economics: AI is not replacing human work uniformly; it is changing which parts of human work are valuable. The implication for individual workers is that the right strategy is to migrate up the verification-and-judgment stack rather than to compete with AI on draft generation. The implication for educational institutions is more disruptive: curricula that emphasize draft generation as a primary skill (introductory writing, introductory programming) need to be substantially rethought.

Practical Example: A Junior Software Engineer In 2026

The job description has shifted measurably. In 2022, a junior engineer's day was 60% writing code, 25% reading code, and 15% meetings. In 2026, it is roughly 30% writing code (with AI assistance), 35% reading and verifying code (some of which the AI generated), 20% writing tests and specifications (which constrain AI generation), and 15% meetings. The hiring criteria adjusted to match: prompt fluency and verification skill matter more than raw syntactic speed. Junior engineers who treat AI as a productivity multiplier are doing well; junior engineers who treat AI as a competitor are not. This pattern generalizes beyond software engineering.

The Productivity Question

Aggregate productivity statistics are the slowest indicator and the most economically important one. Through 2025, U.S. labor productivity (per the Bureau of Labor Statistics productivity series) grew faster than the 2010-2019 trend (roughly 1.8% versus a 1.4% trend), but not dramatically so, and not in the pattern an "AI-driven productivity miracle" reading would predict. Productivity gains are concentrated in industries with high AI adoption (information, professional services), consistent with the augmentation reading, but the magnitude is "noticeable lift" rather than "transformative leap."

This is the cleanest argument against the most extreme AI-economic claims as of 2026. If AI were already producing massive automation gains, aggregate productivity numbers would be substantially higher than the trend line. They are not. This does not refute claims about 2027 or 2030; it does constrain claims about 2025-2026, and it suggests that the labor-market adjustment is happening through workflow change rather than through productivity discontinuity.

Caveat: 2026 Is Not 2030

Every number in this section is a snapshot, and every snapshot is consistent with multiple trajectories. The augmentation-dominant 2025 deployment pattern could continue, in which case 2030 looks like a more productive version of 2025. Or the deployment pattern could flip toward automation as agentic systems become more capable in 2026-2027, in which case 2030 looks substantially more disruptive. The 2.5%-to-7% U.S. employment-at-risk estimates that span the literature reflect this exact uncertainty. The honest reading of 2026 data is that augmentation is currently dominant; the honest reading of 2026 trends is that this could change.

What To Do With This Data

For a founder building AI products, the augmentation-dominant pattern is good news: customers are paying for tools that help their workers, not tools that replace their workers, and the latter is a substantially harder sell. For a policymaker, the data argues for investing in transition support (the kind of skill-shift retraining that the academic literature documents as effective) rather than in absolute employment guarantees that misframe the problem. For a worker, the empirical answer is: learn to use these systems as collaborators; the augmentation pattern is rewarding workers who do this and not rewarding workers who do not.
