AGI Timelines: The 2027-2033 Spectrum

Section 64.3

"When will AGI arrive?" is the most-asked, most-disputed, and most-consequential question in the field, and the answer depends almost entirely on who is being asked. Dario Amodei publicly anchors 2026-27. Demis Hassabis still says approximately 2030. Metaculus's median forecast as of mid-2026 has compressed to March 30, 2028, but its 25%/50% confidence spread (2029 / 2033) reflects real disagreement among forecasters. Polymarket gives only roughly 9% to "AGI by 2027." The forecasters are looking at the same evidence and reaching different conclusions.

This section maps the 2027-2033 spectrum the field actually believes in, the methodological choices behind each region of the spectrum, and the empirical observations from 2025-2026 that should shift those beliefs. The point is not to predict a date; the point is to characterize the disagreement honestly and to identify which kinds of evidence would actually move different camps.

Why The Timeline Debate Matters Operationally

If AGI arrives in 2027, the right strategy is to maximize compute and capability now and worry about adoption later. If AGI arrives in 2033, the right strategy is to build durable systems, evaluation infrastructure, and economic adoption pathways for the technology we have. These are incompatible strategies; you cannot pursue both. Every founder, policymaker, and researcher in this field is implicitly betting on a point on the 2027-2033 spectrum, whether or not they would articulate it that way. Making the bet explicit is the first step to making it well.

The Amodei Frame: 2026-2027

The case for an AGI date in 2026-2027 rests on extrapolating recent capability gains directly. The argument: in 2022 GPT-3.5 could not reliably do high-school math. In 2024 GPT-4 could not reliably solve research-grade math problems. In 2026 frontier models solve 29% of FrontierMath Tier 4. If this trajectory continues at the same slope for another 18-24 months, the resulting system would solve a substantial majority of research-grade problems across STEM domains. Combined with current rates of progress on coding agents and long-horizon planning, this is a system that could plausibly substitute for a competent professional in many domains.

This frame has the virtue of being empirically grounded in recent capability slopes. It has the vulnerability that the slopes do not have to continue; the post-training reasoning paradigm could saturate, the architectural bets could not pan out, and the compute frontier could hit a power or chip-supply wall. The Amodei position bets that the post-training paradigm is far from exhausted and that the compute supply pipeline (Cerebras, AMD MI355, Trainium) will continue to scale. Both are plausible but neither is certain.

The Hassabis Frame: ~2030

The case for AGI around 2030 rests on a different reading of what "general" requires. The argument: current frontier models are extraordinary at language-mediated cognition (writing, code, math) and substantially worse at modalities that involve real-time interaction with a physical or social environment. AGI in any meaningful sense requires competence across all of these, not just the language ones. Closing the embodied, multi-modal, continual-learning gaps requires research progress that is not visible on the 2026 horizon, and "five-ish years" is the honest estimate.

This frame's strength is that it takes the definition of AGI seriously. If "general intelligence" means "as broadly capable as a human," then a system that scores 50% on HLE but cannot plan a week of dependent tasks autonomously, learn from yesterday's mistakes, or operate in a physical environment is not AGI by any reasonable definition. The vulnerability is that "five more years" is also what the field has been saying since 2018. The Hassabis position is robust against premature claims but vulnerable to the criticism that it always recedes by exactly the same five years.

The Metaculus Frame: 2028-2033, With Wide Spread

Metaculus's forecaster community aggregates many predictions and tends to converge on something between the two extremes above. The question that anchors this discussion asks when "the first general AI system" will be devised, tested, and publicly announced; the operationalization includes both capability criteria (solving open math problems, passing a Turing test under specific protocols) and announcement criteria.

The median forecast compressed from approximately 2032 in early 2024 to March 30, 2028 by mid-2026, an unusually rapid update by Metaculus standards. The 25%-75% confidence interval covers roughly 2027 to 2033, which is the title of this section. Forecaster comments are illuminating: many flagged the 2024-2025 reasoning-model paradigm as a substantial update on their priors, while others argued the operationalization criteria are too strict for current systems to meet within the median timeline.

Key Insight: The Spread Is The Point

The fact that responsible forecasters span 2027-2033 with substantial probability mass at both ends tells you something more useful than any point estimate. Six years of uncertainty in a question whose answer determines economic and policy strategy is itself the relevant fact. Strategies that work in expectation across the full spread are more robust than strategies optimized for a specific date. The widely-distributed nature of the forecast is a feature of the underlying epistemic state, not a failure of any particular forecaster.

The Polymarket Frame: Probability-Weighted Skepticism

Prediction markets aggregate financial bets rather than expert opinion, and they often disagree with Metaculus. As of mid-2026, Polymarket markets give roughly 9% probability to AGI by end of 2027. This is substantially lower than the Metaculus implied probability for the same date (which is closer to 25%).

The gap is partly methodological (Polymarket includes traders who do not closely follow AI research; Metaculus skews toward forecasting professionals) and partly definitional (Polymarket markets often operationalize AGI more strictly). It is also partly bias: prediction markets historically underweight tail outcomes that require a large divergence from the status quo, which AGI by 2027 would be. The Polymarket number is best read as "the AGI-skeptic position, weighted by people putting money on it," not as the consensus probability.

The 80,000 Hours Diagnosis

The most lucid summary of what changed in 2025 is the 80,000 Hours essay "What the hell happened with AGI timelines in 2025?". The essay's argument is that 2025 saw a structural update in how the forecasting community thinks about timelines: not because any specific lab released a system that was qualitatively new, but because the reasoning-model paradigm demonstrated a new way for capability to scale (post-training compute rather than pretraining compute), which the previous timeline models had not accounted for. The LessWrong visualization "A visualization of changing AGI timelines, 2023-2026" shows the same compression graphically.

The structural-update reading is important because it suggests the next paradigm shift will also compress timelines. If the field is in a regime where new scaling axes keep emerging (pretraining compute, then post-training compute, then perhaps continual learning or multi-agent reasoning), then "AGI in N years" depends on whether the next axis is discovered within those N years, which is itself a forecasting problem with very wide error bars.

Caveat: AGI Is Not One Thing

Almost every dispute in the timeline debate dissolves on close inspection into a definitional disagreement. Is AGI "a system that beats any expert at any cognitive task"? "A system that can do remote work indefinitely"? "A system whose creators cannot meaningfully outperform it on any task"? Different participants in the debate use different definitions, and the operationalizations matter. Stanford HAI's 2026 predictions writeup is one of the more careful reads, in part because it foregrounds the definitional question. When you read an AGI-timeline claim, the first question to ask is what the speaker means by AGI; the second is what evidence would falsify their claim.

What Would Move The Forecast

The most useful framing is not "when will AGI arrive" but "what evidence in 2026-2027 would shift the forecast left or right." Three observable signals would compress timelines: HLE scores moving above 60% (which would require a fundamentally better reasoning paradigm than current models), a frontier model passing FrontierMath Tier 4 at above 50% (which would represent research-mathematician-level capability becoming reliable), and decentralized training crossing the GPT-4 capability threshold (which would broaden who can produce frontier models). Three signals would lengthen timelines: HLE scores stalling at 45-50% over the next 18 months despite increased reasoning compute, ARC-AGI-3 proving substantially harder than expected even for the best agentic systems, and an alignment incident severe enough to slow deployment of subsequent frontier models.

The honest answer to "when does AGI arrive" is that 2027-2033 is the genuinely-believed range and that the next 12-18 months of capability and alignment progress will shift the distribution within that range. The forecast is not stuck for lack of effort; it is stuck because the underlying evidence is genuinely ambiguous.
