Beyond NVIDIA: Groq, Cerebras, Tenstorrent, AMD MI355

Section 63.1

For a decade, "AI hardware" meant NVIDIA. By mid-2026 that sentence is wrong in two interesting ways. First, NVIDIA still dominates training, but the company itself has bought one of its biggest inference rivals (Groq, acquired December 2025 for roughly $20 billion) and is now shipping a rack-scale product, Vera Rubin LPX, that bolts Groq LPUs alongside its own Blackwell-successor GPUs. Second, the most important inference deal of the year was not an NVIDIA deal at all: in January 2026 OpenAI signed a 750 megawatt, $10B+ supply agreement with Cerebras for wafer-scale silicon. The era of single-vendor AI infrastructure is over.

This section walks through the four serious non-NVIDIA inference and training stories of 2025-2026, what each silicon family is good at, and where the bet behind each one is plausible or speculative. The unifying thread is that inference cost, not training compute, is the wall this hardware is trying to climb over. Every architecture below makes a different bet on how to break the memory-bandwidth bottleneck that limits decoder throughput.

Big Picture

The "scaling wall" framing is in retreat; the new wall is inference cost. A frontier MoE model can be served for fractions of a cent per query on one architecture and several cents on another. Multiply by hundreds of millions of daily requests and the hardware choice becomes the dominant operating expense line. The Cerebras, Groq, Tenstorrent, and AMD MI355 stories are four distinct attempts to move that line. Whichever combination wins shapes who can profitably operate a frontier model in 2027.

Cerebras: Wafer-Scale Goes Mainstream

Cerebras's bet is the most physically extreme in the industry. Instead of cutting silicon wafers into many small chips and connecting them through slow off-chip interconnect, Cerebras keeps the wafer intact. The CS-3 system is a single die roughly the size of a dinner plate with about 900,000 AI cores and 44 GB of on-die SRAM. The economic argument is simple: for inference workloads dominated by memory bandwidth, you want all weights as close to the compute as physically possible, and "on the same wafer" is as close as it gets.

Until 2025, Cerebras was a research-lab oddity. The January 2026 OpenAI deal changed that. A 750 MW commitment over multiple years is hyperscaler-grade demand; it converts wafer-scale silicon from a curiosity into a primary inference fabric. Cerebras then filed for an IPO in early 2026, and the question now is whether the company can ship at hyperscale yields. Wafer-scale defect tolerance is real (the architecture routes around dead cores), but capacity at this physical scale has not been proven outside of a single vendor's pilot fab.

Groq, Now Inside NVIDIA

Groq's bet is the opposite of Cerebras's: keep the chip small, but make it deterministic. The Language Processing Unit (LPU) uses a deeply pipelined, compiler-scheduled dataflow architecture with no caches and no speculative execution. Latency becomes predictable to the cycle, which matters when you are serving a chat agent and need the next token to arrive on a strict budget. Groq's public demos hit single-digit-millisecond first-token latency on Llama-class models, a number that conventional GPU inference stacks cannot match without aggressive batching tricks.

NVIDIA's December 2025 acquisition, at $20B, was the largest single AI-silicon transaction of the year. The integration product is Vera Rubin LPX, a rack that pairs Groq LPUs (relabeled in NVIDIA's lineup) with NVIDIA's training silicon. The strategic question is whether this consolidates the inference market under one roof or accelerates competitors who now have a clear "non-NVIDIA-stack" pitch to enterprise buyers. Both readings have proponents inside and outside the company.

Key Insight: The Two Inference Bets

Cerebras and Groq make opposite physical bets. Cerebras goes huge (one wafer, everything local); Groq goes small but tightly choreographed (compiler-scheduled, no cache). Both are reactions to the same fact: a frontier MoE serving stack on commodity GPUs is dominated by memory-bandwidth stalls, not compute. If you eliminate the bottleneck by colocation (Cerebras) or by determinism (Groq), the rest of the system gets faster. The wrong frame is "which one wins." The interesting frame is "which workloads each is best at," because workloads with high context reuse favor wafer-scale and workloads with strict latency budgets favor deterministic dataflow.

Tenstorrent: RISC-V Chiplets as a Third Road

Tenstorrent, led by Jim Keller, takes neither the wafer-scale nor the deterministic-pipeline route. Its bet is on RISC-V chiplets that can be composed into many topologies, with the entire software stack open. The company raised $700M in December 2024 and has shipped Wormhole boards used by independent labs and universities. The wager is that the AI accelerator market eventually wants a Linux-and-RISC-V analog: an open ISA, an open kernel stack, and many vendors building compatible silicon.

This is a longer bet than Cerebras's or Groq's. As of 2026 Tenstorrent has no hyperscaler design win. But the platform exists; it is shipping; and it is the only viable path for organizations that need on-premises AI silicon without depending on a single proprietary toolchain. Whether 2027 brings a serious enterprise design win for Tenstorrent is one of the open questions of the post-Groq consolidation era.

AMD MI355X: The Conventional Challenger

AMD's Instinct MI355X is the unspectacular but consequential entry. It is a conventional GPU architecture (CDNA family, HBM3e memory) targeted at the same training and inference workloads NVIDIA serves with Blackwell. The bandwidth number that matters is about 6 TB/s of HBM per package, which closes most of the gap to NVIDIA on the workloads where memory bandwidth dominates. The strategic value of the MI355X is less the silicon itself than the existence of a credible second source for hyperscalers placing multi-billion-dollar orders. AWS and Microsoft both qualified MI300 and MI355 parts in 2025.

Adjacent to AMD, the cloud-vertical entries continue: AWS Trainium 2 went generally available in December 2024 and is widely deployed for in-house Amazon workloads; Trainium 4 is expected late 2026. Google's TPU v6 ("Trillium") has been the workhorse for Gemini training. None of these is a public-cloud competitive market the way commodity x86 is, but together they convert "NVIDIA-or-bust" into a multi-vendor procurement decision.

Caveat: TCO is Workload-Specific

None of these silicon options is uniformly cheaper or faster. Cerebras dominates very-large-context single-stream throughput; Groq/LPX dominates short-context low-latency chat; AMD MI355 dominates dense-tensor training; NVIDIA Blackwell still dominates the long tail of mixed workloads. Total cost of ownership analyses that compare them on a single benchmark are almost always misleading. The right question is "which workload profile is my serving stack?" and the right answer is often "we use two vendors and route by profile."

What's next: hardware that is fast at multiplying matrices is necessary but not sufficient if you are still moving petabytes of weights between datacenters. The next section turns to the other half of the systems story, decentralized training, where the central question is not how fast the silicon is but how little gradient bandwidth you can get away with.
