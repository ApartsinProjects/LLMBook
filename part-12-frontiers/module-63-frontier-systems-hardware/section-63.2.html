Decentralized Training: Nous Psyche, DeMo, DisTrO

Section 63.2

For ten years, every credible frontier model was trained in a single datacenter, with thousands of GPUs sitting on a low-latency InfiniBand or NVLink fabric. The reason is not preference; it is bandwidth physics. Standard data-parallel training synchronizes gradients every step, and "every step" at frontier scale means moving tens of gigabytes per second per accelerator. Two GPUs in adjacent racks can do this. Two GPUs in different cities cannot, at least not under the conventional algorithm.

Decentralized training is the line of work that tries to break this constraint. The 2024-2026 progress, driven primarily by Nous Research and a handful of collaborators, has moved the gradient-communication budget down by three to four orders of magnitude. As of 2026 you can train a mid-size LLM with participants connecting over consumer-grade internet links. The largest fully-decentralized run is still below GPT-4-class capability, but the technique has crossed the threshold from theoretical curiosity to working infrastructure, and the implications for who can build frontier models are significant.

Big Picture: Why This Matters Strategically

If frontier training requires colocated hardware, then frontier model production is a hyperscaler-only business. If it does not, then a university consortium, a national lab, or an open community can in principle pool heterogeneous hardware and produce frontier-comparable models. Decentralized training therefore touches geopolitics (export controls become harder to enforce), economics (training is a network good rather than a capex moat), and AI safety (open-community models are less centrally governable). None of these implications are settled by the algorithms alone, but the algorithms had to exist first.

DeMo: Decoupled Momentum Optimization

The algorithmic heart of decentralized training is DeMo (Decoupled Momentum Optimization), introduced by Bowen Peng, Diederik Kingma, and collaborators in 2024 and revised in October 2025. The insight is that during synchronous data-parallel training, most of the gradient information is redundant. Conventional all-reduce sends the full gradient tensor across the wire every step. DeMo decouples the optimizer's momentum state from the gradient transmission, sends only a sparse, top-k decomposition of the most informative components, and reconstructs a momentum update on each node.

The reported communication reduction is striking: between 1,000x and 10,000x less inter-node bandwidth versus standard distributed Adam on the same training run, with negligible quality loss on models tested through ~7B parameters. The mechanism is conceptually similar to gradient compression literature, but where earlier compression schemes lost accuracy at frontier scale, DeMo's coupling to momentum state preserves the optimizer's directional intuition. The combination of momentum decoupling and signed top-k sparsification is what makes DeMo competitive rather than merely cheaper.

Key Insight: Communication Cost Was Mostly Redundant

The standard mental model of distributed training is that "every gradient bit is important; that is why we synchronize all of them." DeMo argues this is wrong. In high-dimensional gradient spaces, the meaningful update direction is concentrated in a small fraction of coordinates per step. Once you send those plus enough state to reconstruct momentum, the rest can be approximated locally without measurable quality loss. The frontier-training community now sees the communication reduction not as a clever trick but as evidence that 99% of conventional all-reduce traffic was always redundant.

DisTrO: The Production Implementation

DisTrO (Distributed Training Over-the-Internet) is the production-grade implementation of DeMo plus the surrounding infrastructure: fault-tolerant coordination, node-churn handling, checkpoint sharding across heterogeneous storage, and a verifiable participation log. The verifiable log is the piece that makes DisTrO interesting beyond pure algorithmic research. It lets a coordinator certify that a given gradient contribution was actually computed by a participating node rather than fabricated. This is the foundation that makes "open-internet training" plausible as a model of governance, not just as a research demo.

DisTrO is the substrate on which Nous Research built Psyche.

Psyche: Decentralized Training on Solana

Psyche Network, launched on Solana in January 2025, is the first production-deployed decentralized training stack. The architecture: nodes contribute compute on their own hardware, gradient updates flow through DisTrO, participation is logged on-chain, and an aggregator coordinates model checkpoints. As of 2026 Psyche has trained mid-size models that are publicly downloadable and that achieve quality competitive with similarly-sized centrally-trained models.

The unresolved question is whether Psyche, or any decentralized stack, can cross the GPT-4 capability threshold. The largest run published as of mid-2026 is a few billion active parameters; that is not yet frontier. The community argues two cases. Optimists point to DeMo v2's bandwidth efficiency and argue that scaling up is now an organizational problem (how do you orchestrate 10,000 volunteer nodes) rather than an algorithmic problem. Pessimists note that training at frontier scale requires extreme stability (loss spikes have to be debuggable), and debugging across an open peer-to-peer network is genuinely harder than debugging in a single datacenter.

Research Frontier: When Does Decentralized Cross The Frontier?

This is the single open question that will define how 2027 looks for AI governance. If a decentralized consortium produces a model competitive with the strongest closed-lab frontier in 2027, then the assumption that frontier AI is concentrated in three or four labs collapses. If decentralized training stalls below GPT-4-class through 2027, then centralization, with all the policy and concentration implications, continues. Chakra Research's overview, "The Third Epoch of AI: Decentralizing the Training Stack", is the best non-technical summary of where the field is and what each scenario would mean.

The Hardware Heterogeneity Problem

One subtlety the decentralized-training literature is still working through is hardware heterogeneity. In a single datacenter, every GPU is the same SKU. In an open network, you have RTX 4090s, A100s, MI300X cards, Apple Silicon, and the occasional H100 cluster. Numerical precision differs (BF16 vs FP16 vs FP8); kernel implementations differ; and the optimizer step has to be reproducible across all of them, or the model diverges.

DeMo v2 partially addresses this by quantizing the transmitted top-k components to integer representations and reconstructing in higher precision locally. But heterogeneity tax is real: a Psyche run that includes ten different GPU generations will train slower per step than one that uses a single SKU, sometimes substantially slower. Whether this tax is fundamental or whether better algorithms can erase it is an active research thread.

What This Means For Practitioners

If you are not running a frontier lab, the immediate implications of decentralized training are mostly indirect: it lowers the cost of producing open-weight base models, which lowers the price you pay for inference of those models. Direct participation is feasible for academic research groups, university consortia, and well-organized open-source communities. Direct participation is not yet feasible for an enterprise that needs production guarantees on training timelines.

The medium-term implication is more interesting. If DeMo-class algorithms continue to reduce the bandwidth gap, the strategic moat of owning a datacenter erodes. The moat that remains is data quality, post-training know-how, and serving infrastructure, not raw training capacity. This is a substantial reshaping of who holds power in the AI value chain.
