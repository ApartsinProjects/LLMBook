Training-Inference Co-Design

Section 63.5

The conventional way to think about LLM development is sequential: a team trains a model, hands it to a serving team, and the serving team's job is to deploy whatever model they were given as efficiently as possible. This division of labor produces a predictable failure mode: pretraining decisions optimize for loss curves that have little to do with inference economics, and inference engineers spend the next year trying to recover throughput that was lost the day the architecture was frozen.

Training-inference co-design is the cluster of techniques that breaks this division. The pretraining team decides the architecture knowing what the serving team will need; the serving team specifies KV-cache budgets, latency targets, and quantization constraints before the first GPU starts training. The 2024-2026 wave of work (Beyond-Chinchilla inference-aware scaling laws, 1.58-bit pretraining, FlashAttention-aware architecture choices) made this co-design the default rather than the exception at frontier labs. This section walks through the load-bearing pieces.

Big Picture: When Architecture Becomes A Serving Decision

The headline claim of the 2024 paper "Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal: Accounting for Inference in LM Scaling Laws" (Sardana et al.) is that the optimal compute split between training and inference depends on how many tokens the model will eventually serve. A model that will serve 100 trillion tokens at inference should be smaller and trained on more data than Chinchilla suggests; a model that will serve very few tokens should be larger and trained on less. Once you start asking "how many tokens at inference?" you are doing co-design, whether you call it that or not.

The Beyond-Chinchilla Argument

The Chinchilla scaling laws from 2022 estimated the compute-optimal balance of parameters and training tokens for a fixed pretraining budget. The hidden assumption was that inference compute is irrelevant. For a research model that ships once and gets evaluated on a benchmark, that assumption is fine. For a production model that will serve a hundred million users every day for two years, it is wrong by orders of magnitude.

Sardana et al. (2024) formalize this. They show that when you account for inference compute, the optimal model becomes substantially smaller than Chinchilla would predict, because every parameter you do not have is a parameter you do not pay to move through HBM at every inference step. The price is paid up front in additional training tokens. Empirically, models trained to 10-20x Chinchilla-optimal token counts (Llama 3, Llama 4, DeepSeek V3) have set the recent quality-per-inference-dollar frontier, vindicating the prediction.

The most extreme version of this argument is Liquid AI's LFM2.5-350M (April 2026), which set a new tokens-to-parameter ratio of 80,000:1 (350M parameters, 28 trillion training tokens). The model is a fraction of a percent the size of a frontier model, by design, and serves at orders of magnitude lower cost. Whether the quality is competitive on hard tasks is a separate question (it is not), but for inference-cost-sensitive deployments the bet is paying off.

1.58-Bit Pretraining As Inference-Aware Architecture

The most dramatic co-design example is the BitNet b1.58 line from Microsoft Research. Standard practice is to train a model in BF16 or FP16, then quantize to 4-bit or 8-bit for inference. The quantization-after-training approach pays a measurable quality cost; the lower the bit-width, the worse the cost.

BitNet inverts the order. The model is pretrained in 1.58 bits per weight (specifically, ternary weights, {-1, 0, +1}) so that the optimizer learns weights that are already quantization-friendly. The cost is an extra ~20% training tokens versus a comparable BF16 model; the benefit is that the deployed model needs no quantization step, takes a fraction of the memory, and can run on CPUs at competitive throughput.

The April 2025 BitNet b1.58 2B4T technical report describes the first open-source, native 1-bit LLM at meaningful (2B parameter) scale. The companion bitnet.cpp framework delivers the throughput needed to make CPU-only deployment practical. The throughput numbers (2.37x to 6.17x x86 CPU speedups, 1.37x to 5.07x ARM) are not incremental; they convert "you need a GPU to run an LLM" into "a laptop CPU is sufficient for a 2B model." The catch is that 1.58-bit pretraining at frontier scale (70B+ parameters with multi-trillion-token training and reasoning-style post-training) is not yet demonstrated. The architecture works at 2B; it has to work at 70B for the bet to pay off at the frontier.

Research Frontier: Does BitNet Survive Reasoning RL?

The 2025 reasoning paradigm relies on long RL post-training runs (RLVR, GRPO) that modify the base model substantially. There is no published evidence yet that 1.58-bit weights remain stable through this post-training step at frontier scale. The optimizer dynamics in long RL post-training have a different signature than supervised pretraining, and the small per-weight precision budget that suffices for the latter may not suffice for the former. If 1.58-bit weights de-quantize during RL post-training (require restoration to higher precision to remain trainable), the entire economic argument for BitNet collapses for frontier reasoning models. This is the single most important open empirical question in the inference-aware-architecture cluster.

Architecture Choices Shaped By Kernels

FlashAttention-class kernels, covered in Section 63.4, do not just optimize a fixed architecture; they reshape what architectures are economical. Multi-Query Attention (MQA) and Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) became standard partly because they reduce KV-cache size; they only became standard once kernels that exploit the reduction (FlashAttention-2 and later) were widely available. DeepSeek V3 and V4's Compressed Sparse Attention is another example: the architecture was chosen for its KV-cache compression properties, which only pay off because the inference kernel for it was co-designed with the architecture.

Mixture-of-Experts is the same story at a coarser grain. MoE makes serving cheaper because only a fraction of weights activate per token, but it makes the inference pipeline more complex because routing decisions have to be made every layer. MoE only became the dominant frontier architecture once routing kernels could keep up with the algorithmic possibility; Qwen3-235B-A22B's 128 experts with top-8 routing would not be servable economically without the corresponding kernel infrastructure.

Apple's End-to-End Co-Design

Apple's 2026 ICLR paper "Native LLM and MLLM Inference at Scale on Apple Silicon" is the cleanest published example of training-inference co-design as an institutional process. The paper describes how Apple's Foundation Models team chose architecture, quantization scheme, and training recipe specifically so the resulting model could be served efficiently on iPhone-class hardware with unified memory. The architectural choices (model dimensions, attention configuration, FFN structure) are explicitly justified by the constraints of the target inference platform.

The institutional point is more interesting than any individual choice. At Apple, the model team and the silicon team negotiate as peers; neither hands a finished artifact to the other. This is the organizational shape that co-design implies, and it is part of why Apple's on-device models perform as well as they do on hardware that has a fraction of a datacenter GPU's raw FLOPs.

Key Insight: Co-Design Is An Org Design

Training-inference co-design is, at root, a coordination problem between teams that historically had different mandates. The labs that do it well (Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepSeek, Apple) all share a structural pattern: the inference team is consulted on architecture decisions before the pretraining run starts, and the inference team's projections of cost-per-token are a first-class input alongside loss-curve projections. The labs that do it badly have a serving team that finds out about architectural choices when the checkpoint arrives. The technical literature documents the algorithms; the org-chart pattern is what makes them work.

Where The Pattern Goes Next

Three directions are visible in early 2026. First, post-training-aware architecture: the field is starting to ask "what architecture is best for RL post-training" rather than just "what architecture pretrains well." Reasoning models burn enormous test-time compute, and the architecture that minimizes RL-post-training cost is not necessarily the same as the one that minimizes pretraining cost. Second, hardware-aware MoE: routing decisions in MoE have a strong interaction with the memory hierarchy of the target accelerator, and serving-stack-aware routing is an active research thread. Third, batch-aware quantization: different requests in a single batch may benefit from different precision, and the kernel-level cost of mixed-precision batching is starting to come down.
