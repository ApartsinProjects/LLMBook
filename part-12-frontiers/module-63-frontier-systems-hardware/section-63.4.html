FlashAttention-4 and Inference Kernels for Blackwell

Section 63.4

Of all the load-bearing kernels in modern LLM inference, the attention computation is the one whose performance most directly determines whether a model is profitable to serve. The original FlashAttention paper (2022) made attention IO-aware, which roughly tripled training throughput on long sequences. FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 refined the technique for Hopper-class GPUs. In March 2026 the FlashAttention-4 paper dropped, and the headline result is that Tri Dao and collaborators have once again rewritten the attention kernel from scratch for the new hardware reality: Blackwell-class GPUs with asymmetric tensor cores and a different memory hierarchy than anything that came before.

This section explains what is genuinely new in FlashAttention-4, why Blackwell required a redesign rather than a port, and how the algorithm-kernel co-design pattern in this work generalizes to the rest of the 2026 inference stack. The reason this matters to a non-kernel-author reader is that the kernel choice determines what context length, what batch size, and what model size are economically serviceable; in 2026 these decisions cascade up into product strategy.

Why The Kernel Layer Decides Inference Economics

Transformer inference cost is, to first order, the cost of moving the KV cache through the matrix-multiply units. The attention kernel is the code that decides how that movement happens. A 30% kernel improvement at the attention layer is a 30% reduction in the dollar cost per query, which at hyperscale is hundreds of millions of dollars per year per major lab. This is why FlashAttention-class work draws engineering attention out of proportion to its line count. The bandwidth-vs-compute balance in this kernel is more economically consequential than most architectural choices above it.

What Changed With Blackwell

NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture (B200 and successors) introduced two changes that broke the FlashAttention-3 design. First, the tensor cores are asymmetric: the compute throughput for FP4 and FP8 ops outpaces what the surrounding memory hierarchy can feed unless software explicitly pipelines the data movement. Second, the shared memory hierarchy is larger and faster but is laid out differently from Hopper, which means the tile sizes that were optimal on H100 are leaving performance on the table on B200.

The pre-FlashAttention-4 state of the world: many shipping serving stacks were using FlashAttention-3 kernels on Blackwell with hand-tuned tile sizes. This works, but leaves roughly 30-40% throughput on the table relative to a fresh design. Together AI's writeup and Modal Labs' reverse-engineering analysis both characterize the gap as "the obvious next 10-30% if anyone bothered to redo the kernel from scratch." FlashAttention-4 is that redesign.

Algorithm-Kernel Pipelining Co-Design

The key technical idea in FlashAttention-4, captured in the paper's subtitle "Algorithm and Kernel Pipelining Co-Design for Asymmetric Hardware Scaling," is that the algorithm itself is restructured to feed the hardware pipeline. Earlier FlashAttention versions treated the attention math as fixed and optimized the data movement around it. FlashAttention-4 lets the math be slightly different in order to make the pipeline easier to fill.

Concretely, FlashAttention-4 reorders the softmax computation and uses a different accumulation scheme so that the tensor core, the load engine, and the writeback path all stay busy simultaneously rather than waiting on each other. The numerical result is identical to within standard-floating-point error, but the throughput on B200 is roughly 1.5-1.7x FlashAttention-3 on long contexts. On short contexts the gain is smaller, because the bottleneck moves to issue rate rather than data movement.

Key Insight: When Math Becomes Negotiable

The implicit assumption in most kernel work is that you cannot touch the mathematics; you can only change how it is computed. FlashAttention-4 inverts this. It accepts a small reordering of the attention computation (algebraically equivalent, numerically equivalent within tolerance) in exchange for a kernel that fits the hardware better. This is a pattern: at the frontier of inference performance, you no longer optimize a fixed algorithm; you co-design algorithm and kernel so they fit each other. Expect this pattern to spread from attention to other transformer kernels (MoE routing, MLP fusion) over 2026-2027.

The Inference Hardware Survey

FlashAttention-4 is the most cited specific kernel paper of 2026, but the broader literature is also worth tracking. The January 2026 survey "Challenges and Research Directions for LLM Inference Hardware" is the canonical reference for where inference-hardware research is going beyond attention. Its taxonomy covers: KV-cache compression, speculative decoding kernels, MoE routing kernels (which have become a bottleneck as expert counts climb), prefill-decode disaggregation, and disaggregated memory hierarchies. The "Large Language Model Inference Acceleration: A Comprehensive Hardware Perspective" review covers similar ground from a more hardware-centric angle.

The pattern that emerges from both surveys is that the attention layer is no longer the only kernel anyone optimizes. As MoE models with hundreds of experts became standard (DeepSeek V4, Qwen3-235B), the routing layer became its own performance hotspot. The expert-dispatch kernel matters as much as the attention kernel on these models, and it is currently less well-optimized.

Multi-Stage Pipeline: HERMES

Adjacent to single-kernel work, MIT's HERMES (2025) is the reference work on understanding and optimizing multi-stage inference pipelines. The HERMES contribution is to model an entire serving pipeline (input tokenization, prefill, generation, postprocessing) as a sequence of stages with different bottlenecks and to identify the cross-stage optimizations that single-kernel work cannot find. For example: prefill is compute-bound, generation is memory-bound, and serving both on the same GPU has been shown to waste roughly 30% of either-or capacity. HERMES proposes disaggregating them and demonstrates substantial throughput gains.

This is the same disaggregation pattern that hyperscalers like Anthropic and Google now use in production: prefill on one set of accelerators, generation on another, KV-cache shuttled between them over high-bandwidth interconnect. The economics of this depend on the kernel level (efficient KV transfer kernels are the bottleneck) and on the algorithm level (which models are willing to be split this way without quality loss).

Practical Example: What FA-4 Buys You In Production

Consider a serving stack at 100M requests per day on a single frontier model. Conservatively, attention dominates 30% of inference compute. A 60% kernel speedup at that layer translates to an 18% drop in total cost per query, which on the cited workload is roughly $5-10M per year in saved compute. This is why the inference-kernel literature commands the attention it does: a single paper that closes 18% of total inference cost is, in dollar terms, a more consequential result than most product-feature releases.

What This Means For The Rest Of The Stack

FlashAttention-4 is a Blackwell kernel. It is not portable to AMD MI355 or Cerebras CS-3 without a comparable engineering effort to re-derive the algorithm for those hardware constraints. As of 2026, AMD has its own attention kernels in ROCm; Cerebras has a custom dataflow attention; Groq's LPU has a deterministic-pipeline attention. None of these are equivalent in throughput to the latest FlashAttention version, which means that the NVIDIA performance lead at the kernel level continues to be a real advantage even as the silicon-level competition heats up.

The strategic read: the hardware competition described in Section 63.1 will deliver alternative silicon, but the kernel-software gap is non-trivial and is widening, not narrowing. A non-NVIDIA accelerator with 80% of the raw FLOPS of a Blackwell can still serve at 50% of the effective throughput because the kernel layer has not yet caught up. This is the most underappreciated reason why NVIDIA's dominance has been stickier than the silicon-comparison tables alone would predict.

What's next: improvements at any single layer (silicon, network, kernel) are bounded. The largest 2026 throughput gains came from optimizing across layers simultaneously. The final section of this chapter looks at training-inference co-design, where decisions made during pretraining are deliberately shaped by what the inference stack will do later.
