Front Matter

What This Book Covers

"The best way to learn a complex technology is not to study it in isolation, but to build something real with it, then understand why it works."

Sage, Learning by Building AI Agent

Twelve parts trace a single arc: from the math under one attention head to the strategy for deciding which problems are worth solving with LLMs at all. The early parts develop the substrate; the middle parts develop the working LLM stack; the later parts develop the systems, the safety, and the strategy you need to ship into a real organization. Twenty-one reference appendices sit alongside as the toolbox.

The shape of the book

Parts I-II are the substrate (math, tokenization, the transformer, pretraining, inference, interpretability). Parts III-VI are the working stack (APIs, prompts, training, retrieval, conversation, agents). Parts VII-IX are the systems half (multimodal, evaluation, production, safety). Parts X-XII are the strategy and frontier half (idea-to-product, industry applications, open research questions). Material from 2024-2026 is woven into the relevant chapter rather than gated behind a "recent advances" appendix.

The Twelve Parts

  1. Part I: Foundations. Six chapters that build the substrate. PyTorch and classical ML, NLP and text representation, tokenization and BPE, sequence models, the attention mechanism, the transformer architecture (with a 300-line from-scratch lab), and decoding strategies including the new diffusion-based language models. By the end of Chapter 4 you will have built a working transformer block in PyTorch.
  2. Part II: Understanding LLMs. Six chapters on what makes LLMs work at scale. Pre-training and scaling laws, the modern model landscape (frontier closed models, open-weight families, small language models), reasoning models and test-time compute (DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini, Claude Extended Thinking), inference optimization (quantization, KV caching, speculative decoding), and interpretability (probing, attention attribution, sparse autoencoders). You will reproduce scaling-law predictions and benchmark quantization on a real model.
  3. Part III: Working with LLMs. Three chapters on the day-to-day API surface. Structured output and function calling across providers, prompt engineering with automated optimization via DSPy, and hybrid ML+LLM architectures for when an LLM is not the right tool. You will build a production prompt-management system with multi-provider failover.
  4. Part IV: Training and Adapting. Five chapters on customizing models. Synthetic data generation, fine-tuning fundamentals, parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, QLoRA, adapters), distillation and merging, and the alignment stack (RLHF, DPO, KTO, IPO, preference modeling). You will LoRA-tune a 7B model on domain data and train a reward model.
  5. Part V: Retrieval and Conversation. Three chapters on grounding LLMs in external knowledge. Embeddings and vector databases, retrieval-augmented generation (including long-context tradeoffs and GraphRAG), and full conversational AI systems with voice and real-time streaming. You will ship a document QA pipeline that retrieves, re-ranks, and synthesizes answers from your own corpus.
  6. Part VI: Agentic AI. Five chapters that turn LLMs into systems that take actions. Agent foundations (the ReAct loop, memory architectures, planning), tool use and protocols (function calling, MCP, A2A, AG-UI), multi-agent orchestration, specialized agents (code, browser, research), and agent safety and production deployment. You will build a multi-agent system where a supervisor delegates to specialized workers coordinating through shared state.
  7. Part VII: Multimodal Generation. Three chapters on models that hear, see, move, and imagine. Image generation (Imagen 4, FLUX.1 Pro, SD 3.5), audio and music (Suno v4, ElevenLabs), video (Veo 3, Sora 2, Runway Gen-4), document understanding, unified omni-architectures (GPT-5-omni, Gemini 2 Pro Vision, Llama 4 Scout), vision-language-action models for robotics (OpenVLA, pi-0.5), world models (Genie 3, V-JEPA 2), and 3D Gaussian Splatting as the neural scene representation.
  8. Part VIII: Evaluation and Production. Three chapters on shipping. Evaluation and quality metrics (LLM-as-judge debiasing, long-context benchmarks, OpenTelemetry for LLMs, statistical rigor), LLMOps and deployment engineering (gateways, model routing, durable workflow orchestration, Kubernetes-native serving, edge deployment), and the production toolchain.
  9. Part IX: Safety, Security and Ethics. Three chapters on what can go wrong. LLM security threats (prompt injection, exfiltration, supply-chain attacks), hallucination detection, bias and fairness, regulation (EU AI Act in practice, US frameworks), risk governance and audit, machine unlearning, red teaming with Garak and PyRIT, agent safety, sandboxed execution, and the privacy-tech stack.
  10. Part X: Idea to Product. The entrepreneur's operating model. Eleven chapters on finding LLM-worthy problems, product management with eval-driven specs, build-vs-buy strategy and vendor evaluation, vibe-coding prototypes (Cursor, Claude Code, Cline), MVP design, compute planning, scaling economics and unit costs, shipping, and post-launch iteration. Reads as a standalone playbook.
  11. Part XI: Applications Across Industries. Ten chapters of vertical depth. Legal (contract review, e-discovery), finance (research synthesis, compliance), healthcare and biomedical (ambient documentation, clinical decision support, drug discovery, FDA and HIPAA), education (tutoring, assessment), cybersecurity (SOC automation, blue and red team), government and public sector, manufacturing and supply chain, creative industries, and LLM-powered recommendation and search.
  12. Part XII: Frontiers. Five chapters on what is not yet settled. Emergent abilities, scaling frontiers, alternative architectures (state-space models, hybrid attention, the post-transformer landscape), formal theories of reasoning, memory as a computational primitive, mechanistic interpretability at scale, frontier silicon (Groq, Cerebras, Tenstorrent, AMD MI355), decentralized training, AGI benchmarks (HLE, ARC-AGI-2, FrontierMath), alignment at frontier scale, and the 2027-2033 timeline debate.

Twenty-One Reference Appendices

The appendices are organized into five groups. Foundations (A: Mathematical Foundations, B: ML Essentials) covers prerequisite math and learning theory. Framework Guides (C: HuggingFace, D: LangChain, E: Orchestration Frameworks, F: Agent Frameworks, G: Problem-Solution Key) is the hands-on tooling reference. R&D Infrastructure (H: Python for LLMs, I: Environment Setup, J: Git/DVC, K: Experiment Tracking) covers the daily toolchain. Production Infrastructure (L: Inference Serving, M: Data Engineering, N: Distributed ML, O: MLOps, P: Docker and Containers) covers the deployment side. For Instructors (Q: Course Syllabi, R: Reading Pathways, S: Intermediate Projects, T: Capstone Project, U: War Stories) is the pedagogical kit, including five tested course tracks and named production failures with discussion prompts.

How Concepts Build on Each Other

Think of the book as a city. Parts I-II are infrastructure: the roads and power lines without which the rest cannot function. Part III is the center where everyday work happens (calling APIs, writing prompts, hybrid ML). From there, three neighborhoods branch out in parallel. Part IV (Training) is the factory district. Part V (Retrieval) is the library. Part VI (Agents) is where models take actions. Each branches back to Part III rather than depending on the others, so you can visit in any order. Parts VII-XII then build on one or more of those branches.

What This Book Does Not Cover

This is not a mathematics textbook. The book develops mathematical intuition where needed (attention scores, loss landscapes, scaling-law fits) but does not provide formal proofs or deep statistical learning theory. Readers who want that should pair this book with Bishop's Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning or Goodfellow et al.'s Deep Learning. This is also not a beginner Python tutorial. The book assumes you can write functions, work with dictionaries and lists, install packages, and navigate a terminal. Classical software engineering topics (databases, web frameworks, networking) appear only where they directly intersect with LLM system design.
