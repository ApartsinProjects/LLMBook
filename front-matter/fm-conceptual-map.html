Conceptual Map of This Book

Most LLM books are catalogs of techniques. The techniques change every six months. The principles behind them do not.

This book.
Why this page exists

Modern LLM systems combine prediction, retrieval, tools, memory, evaluation, and human steering into adaptive intelligence workflows. Each of those components is the subject of one or more chapters in this book. But a list of components is not a mental model. This page is the missing intellectual spine: three recurring tensions that drive every design decision in the field, and five theses that the rest of the book is an argument for. Read this page once before Chapter 0; refer back to it whenever a chapter feels like it stands alone.

The Three Tensions

Almost every architectural and engineering choice in LLM systems is a working-out of one of three tensions. Once you can name them, you can predict why a method exists, why it has the form it does, and where it is likely to fail.

Tension 1: Expressiveness vs. Learnability

A model with infinite capacity could in principle represent any function. In practice, more capacity needs more data, more compute, and more careful regularization to avoid memorization without generalization. Every architectural choice (parameter count, depth vs. width, attention vs. recurrence, dense vs. sparse) is a bet about how much expressiveness can be made learnable with the data and compute at hand.

Where this tension shows up: Chapter 4 (transformer design choices), Chapter 6 (scaling laws and Chinchilla optimality), Chapter 33 (Mamba/SSM as alternatives that shift the tradeoff), Chapter 15 (full fine-tuning) and Chapter 16 (PEFT, which buys learnability with parameter efficiency).

Tension 2: Flexibility vs. Controllability

The more capable a model becomes, the more behaviors it exhibits, the harder it is to constrain those behaviors to the ones you want. Pretraining gives flexibility; alignment and prompting attempt to claw back controllability. Every safety, alignment, and evaluation method exists because raw capability does not deliver the behavior you actually want.

Where this tension shows up: Chapter 12 (prompting as the cheapest control mechanism), Chapter 17 (alignment as objective correction), Chapter 25 (agent safety), Chapter 28 (evaluation as checking that controls hold), Chapter 30 (safety and regulation as societal-level controllability).

Tension 3: Knowledge Breadth vs. Knowledge Currency

A model trained on a snapshot of the world contains everything in that snapshot, but knows nothing newer. A retrieval system can give the model access to anything written after training, but cannot integrate new information into the parametric weights. The field has invented at least four distinct solutions (parametric knowledge, RAG, long context, agent memory) precisely because this tension cannot be solved, only navigated.

Where this tension shows up: Chapter 6 (pretraining as knowledge compression), Chapter 15 (continued pretraining vs. forgetting), Chapter 19 (RAG as externalized memory), Chapter 9 (long-context serving as the bandwidth alternative), Chapter 21 (agent memory as persistent context).

The Five Theses

The three tensions explain why methods exist. The five theses below explain how they relate. Each thesis appears as a recurring callout (thesis-thread) throughout the book whenever a section is an instance of one of the five patterns.

Thesis 1: The Compression-Communication Tradeoff

An LLM is a learned compression function over human knowledge. Every architectural and training decision is a bet about what to compress, what to discard, and how to communicate the compressed representation at inference time. Tokenization compresses characters; attention compresses context into fixed computation; scaling laws describe compression quality as a function of compute; RAG is a decision not to compress and to retrieve instead; RLHF compresses human preference into a learned reward signal; agents externalize compression to tools. Where you see this thesis: Chapters 2, 4, 6, 16, 18, 20, 31.

Thesis 2: The Proxy Problem (Goodhart's Law in LLMs)

Every failure mode in this book is a case of optimizing the wrong proxy. Next-token prediction is a proxy for intelligence. Reward model scores are a proxy for helpfulness. Benchmark accuracy is a proxy for real-world capability. Perplexity is a proxy for quality. Attention weights are a proxy for explanation. Each proxy fails in its own way (overfitting, distributional shift, insensitivity, unfaithfulness). The history of the field is a series of better proxies, each with its own failure mode. Where you see this thesis: Chapter 17 (reward hacking), Chapter 19 (citation hallucination), Chapter 28 (benchmark saturation), Chapter 30 (safety failures), Chapter 10 (attention is not causation), Chapter 33 (emergent-ability metric mirage).

Thesis 3: Two Compute Axes × Two Knowledge Types

The field has discovered two orthogonal scaling axes (train-time and test-time) and two orthogonal knowledge types (parametric and non-parametric). Every practical system is a point in a four-dimensional space defined by these axes. Choosing where to be in that space is the fundamental engineering decision, and most "should I use X or Y" questions in LLM systems are really "where in this space should my workload sit?". Where you see this thesis: Chapter 6 (train-time scaling), Chapter 8 (test-time scaling), Chapter 9 (inference: deploying within the chosen point), Chapter 19 (RAG as non-parametric knowledge), Chapter 21 (agents as the test-time compute multiplier).

Thesis 4: Alignment as the Gap Between Objective and Intent

The training objective (next-token prediction over a corpus) is not the deployment objective (helpfulness, safety, truthfulness, accuracy). The entire post-training literature (SFT, RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI, RLVR) is a sequence of attempts to bridge this gap. The gap is not an accident; it is structurally necessary because human intent cannot be fully formalized (Arrow's impossibility theorem applies). Alignment methods can narrow the gap but cannot close it. Where you see this thesis: Chapter 12 (prompting as soft alignment), Chapter 14 (synthetic data as preference shaping), Chapter 17 (RLHF and DPO), Chapter 25 (agent safety as run-time alignment), Chapter 30 (regulation as institutional alignment).

Thesis 5: The Modularity Hypothesis

LLMs behave as if they have modular structure that was not explicitly designed. Attention heads specialize into circuits (induction heads, name movers, indirect-object identifiers). Experts in MoE models specialize by domain. Fine-tuning updates specific knowledge rather than all knowledge. RAG separates retrieval from generation by design choice. Agents separate planning from execution. This modularity emerges from optimization, not architecture. Understanding when and why it emerges is the field's deepest open question. Where you see this thesis: Chapter 4 (residual stream as modular substrate), Chapter 6 (MoE expert specialization), Chapters 14-15 (fine-tuning specificity), Chapter 19 (RAG as designed modularity), Chapter 21 (agents as composed modules), Chapter 10 (mechanistic interpretability as modularity discovery), Chapter 33 (Mamba as a different modular architecture).

How to Use This Map While Reading

Three suggestions:

  1. When a chapter introduces a method: ask which of the three tensions it is trying to navigate. The author will not always say so explicitly. Naming the tension yourself is the most reliable way to remember why the method exists six months from now when the specific library has been deprecated.
  2. When you see a thesis-thread callout: pause and re-read the previous page in light of the named thesis. The callout says "this is an instance of Thesis N." If you cannot see why, the spine is the point at which to push back. The callouts are the connective tissue between local technical content and the field's structural patterns.
  3. When you finish a chapter: ask yourself which tension was at work and which thesis was developed. If the chapter feels like a list of techniques rather than an argument, return here and re-read the relevant thesis. The book is designed so that this question always has an answer.
The Single-Sentence Version

If you only remember one sentence from this map: modern LLM systems are programmable cognitive infrastructures that combine learned prediction (compressed parametric knowledge), external context (retrieval and tools), action (agents), and feedback (alignment and evaluation) to navigate the irreducible tensions between expressiveness, controllability, and currency. Every chapter is an instance of this sentence.
