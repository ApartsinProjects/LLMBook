Robotics, Embodied AI & Scientific Discovery

Section 32.8

A self-driving laboratory is a robot that does science. It plans an experiment, runs it on physical apparatus, measures the outcome, updates a model, and decides what to try next. The 2023-2025 wave of autonomous labs (A-Lab for materials, ChemCrow for chemistry, the privately operated oncology platforms at Insitro and Schrodinger) showed that the data loop, predict, design, measure, retrain, is feasible end to end. They also showed that the bottleneck is no longer compute or LLM capability: it is wet-lab throughput, calibration, and the willingness of human scientists to trust a closed-loop system.

This section maps that landscape. It walks through three reference platforms, surveys the FunSearch algorithm-discovery work that runs the same loop without physical hardware, and closes with the data-loop framing that ties the whole subfield together. The connection back to Chapter 26 (Agents) is direct: an autonomous lab is the agent loop with experiments as tools.

32.8.1 A-Lab: a materials-discovery autonomous laboratory

A-Lab (Berkeley, published in Nature, November 2023) is the most public reference. Over 17 days of unsupervised operation it synthesized 41 novel inorganic materials, hitting a 71% success rate on synthesis attempts proposed by an ML pipeline. The hardware: an articulated robot arm, a high-temperature furnace, X-ray diffraction for characterization, a powder dispenser, and a fume hood. The software: a literature-mining LLM proposes synthesis recipes; a Bayesian optimizer picks which ones to try; a vision model reads the XRD output; the result feeds back into the model. The lesson the field took from A-Lab: the bottleneck is not the LLM's chemistry knowledge but the physical reliability of the hardware. Half the failures were "the robot couldn't transfer the powder cleanly".

32.8.2 ChemCrow: an LLM that uses chemistry tools

ChemCrow (EPFL, 2023, extended through 2024) is the software-only analog. It is a GPT-4-class LLM wired to 17 chemistry tools (RDKit, ChemSpace, retrosynthesis planners, safety-check databases). Given a goal like "synthesize a novel insect repellent", ChemCrow plans a retrosynthesis, checks safety, proposes specific steps, and emits a procedure a human chemist can execute. It does not have a robot; it has tool calls. The interesting move: ChemCrow demonstrated that an LLM that knows when to delegate to a calculator outperformed monolithic chemistry LLMs by margins large enough to publish in Nature Machine Intelligence. The pattern (small model, many tools, careful orchestration) became the template for science-domain LLM applications across drug discovery, climate modeling, and protein design.

32.8.3 FunSearch: discovering algorithms with an evolutionary LLM loop

FunSearch (DeepMind, Nature 2024) takes the data loop into a domain with no physical apparatus at all: pure mathematics and combinatorics. An LLM proposes Python functions intended to solve a stated optimization problem (for instance: lower bounds on cap-set size in finite geometry). An automated evaluator runs each function on a fixed test set and ranks them. The top-scoring programs become the seeds for the next generation, which the LLM mutates. After tens to hundreds of thousands of generations, FunSearch produced provably new lower bounds on previously open mathematical problems and a novel bin-packing algorithm with better worst-case behavior than known heuristics. The take-home: any domain where you can write an automatic scorer is now eligible for closed-loop LLM-driven search.

The data loop is one architecture, three substrates

A-Lab loops over physical synthesis. ChemCrow loops over tool-mediated chemistry knowledge. FunSearch loops over mathematical evaluation. The shape is the same: propose, evaluate, rank, propose again. Once you see the architecture, the question of "what science can autonomous AI do" reduces to "for what task can I write an automatic scorer". The instrumentation problem is harder than the AI problem.

32.8.5 The data-loop view

Every autonomous-science system reduces to four steps. Predict: the model proposes the most informative next experiment. Design: the proposal becomes a concrete protocol (synthesis recipe, function code, drug screen). Measure: the experiment runs and yields data. Retrain (or update beliefs Bayesian-style): the new data improves the model. The loop length determines the science: A-Lab cycles in hours, ChemCrow in seconds (per tool call), FunSearch in milliseconds, oncology labs in weeks. The faster the loop, the more aggressive the search; the slower the loop, the more careful each proposed experiment has to be.

Calibration eats the schedule

Every public autonomous-lab post-mortem reports the same lesson: the LLM is rarely the limiting factor. The robot's repeatability, the sensor drift, the chemistry of unexpected side products, the calibration of the XRD analyzer, these dominate. Budget 60 to 80% of project time for instrumentation engineering and only 10 to 20% for model work. The teams that ship are the ones that hired domain experimentalists alongside ML researchers.

32.8.6 Where embodied AI meets scientific discovery

The frontier in 2026 is multi-modal autonomous labs that combine VLA-driven robot manipulation with LLM-driven hypothesis generation in a single closed loop. Covariant's warehouse manipulation work and Physical Intelligence's generalist policies are the manipulation side; Atomic AI's wet-lab automation and Emerald Cloud Lab's remote-experiment infrastructure are the scaling side. The convergence point: a system where the same VLA can move a pipette, the same dispatcher can plan an experiment, and the same evaluator can rank results. None of the parts are new; the engineering integration is what is.

Fun note

FunSearch's bin-packing algorithm beat the previous best heuristic by 0.32% on a standard benchmark. That margin is rounding error in most contexts; on a global logistics network it is hundreds of millions of dollars of warehouse space. The economically interesting frontier of autonomous-science AI is not the spectacular breakthroughs but the unglamorous tail of "0.3% improvement on a problem run a trillion times a year".
