3D Asset Generation & Neural Scenes

Section 32.5
Big Picture

3D asset generation has crossed the threshold from research demo to production tool. Where Section 32.3 covered neural scene representations as a way to capture reality, this section flips the arrow: starting from text or a single photograph, generative models now hallucinate plausible meshes, textures, and entire interactive worlds. The same diffusion and flow-matching techniques that produced photorealistic 2D images in Section 31.1 have been lifted into voxel grids, triangle soups, and structured latent representations, while world simulators like Genie 3 generate playable environments from a single prompt. The implication for game studios, VFX houses, and robotics labs is roughly the same as text-to-image had for graphic design: the cost floor for "an asset that exists" has collapsed.

Prerequisites

This section assumes familiarity with diffusion models from Section 31.1, the neural scene representations introduced in Section 32.3 (NeRF, Gaussian Splatting), and the latent diffusion concept of operating in compressed feature space. Familiarity with world models from Section 32.4 helps connect 3D generation to the broader embodied AI stack.

For most of the last decade, generating a 3D asset meant either modeling it by hand in Blender, capturing it from many photographs with photogrammetry, or training a small object-specific neural field. None of these scale. Hand modeling is slow and expensive, photogrammetry needs hundreds of well-lit views, and per-object neural fields are useful for visualization but not for building a million-asset library. The breakthrough of the past two years is that text-to-3D and image-to-3D models now generate usable assets in seconds, with quality that game studios are starting to ship in production rather than discard as concept art.

The technical story has two threads. The first is "lift 2D priors into 3D": given a strong text-to-image diffusion model, can we sample views consistently enough across a sphere of cameras to triangulate into geometry? The second is "train directly on 3D": given a large enough dataset of meshes or Gaussian splats, can we run a diffusion process in 3D-native space? Both threads converged in late 2024 and 2025, and the field is now firmly past the "interesting paper" phase and into the "ship it" phase.

32.5.1 Image-to-3D: Stable Zero123 and Multi-View Diffusion

The cleanest entry point is single-image 3D reconstruction. Stable Zero123, released by Stability AI, takes a single photograph plus a target camera pose and synthesizes what the object would look like from that new angle. Run it across a sphere of viewpoints, feed those synthetic views into a NeRF or Gaussian Splatting optimizer, and you get a full 3D model from one photo. The trick that makes this work is that Stable Zero123 is fine-tuned from a 2D diffusion model on the Objaverse-XL dataset of 10 million 3D objects, so its synthesized views are geometrically consistent rather than each being an independent hallucination.

Key Insight

Multi-view consistency is the bottleneck. Single-image diffusion is good at producing one beautiful image. Producing 36 views of the same object that all agree on geometry, lighting, and material is much harder, because every independent sample drifts. Modern 3D generators solve this by making the diffusion process itself condition on all views simultaneously, typically through cross-attention between view tokens. This is the same trick that turned chaotic 2D animation into temporally stable video in Section 31.2: shared attention across what would otherwise be independent samples.

32.5.2 Direct 3D Diffusion: Trellis and Structured Latents

The other branch trains a diffusion model directly in a 3D-native representation. Microsoft's Trellis is the best 2025-era example. Instead of generating images and triangulating, Trellis denoises a structured latent representation (SLAT) that can be decoded into Radiance Fields, 3D Gaussians, or polygon meshes through three separate decoders. A single 2-billion-parameter model thus produces all three formats from one inference pass, which matters because game engines want meshes, real-time rendering wants Gaussians, and physics simulators want both.

The architecture is a rectified flow transformer (the same flow-matching technique that powers Flux in Section 31.1) operating on a sparse voxel grid. Each occupied voxel carries a latent vector; empty voxels are skipped entirely. This sparsity is what makes 1024 by 1024 by 1024 voxel resolutions tractable on a single A100, where a dense grid would require petabytes of memory. The model trains on roughly 500,000 high-quality 3D objects, which is small compared to the billions of images that power Stable Diffusion, but large enough that quality has crossed the threshold where artists incorporate outputs directly rather than as rough drafts.

Real-World Scenario: 3D Asset Pipelines in Production

A mid-size mobile game studio with five 3D artists used to ship one new environment pack per month. With a Trellis-style image-to-3D pipeline plugged into their asset workflow, the same team now ships three to four packs per month, with artists spending their time on retopology, hand-tuning the hero assets, and curating which generated meshes pass the LOD and collision-budget checks. The generator does not replace the artists; it shifts them from "model from scratch" to "edit, approve, optimize." The same pattern is appearing at Roblox, Unity's AI asset marketplace, and at studios building VR worlds where filling a 10-square-kilometer scene by hand was never going to happen.

32.5.3 Generative World Simulators: Imagen 4 World and Genie 3

Beyond single objects, a new class of models generates entire interactive worlds. Google's Genie 2 (and its 2025 successor Genie 3) takes a single still image and turns it into a playable 3D environment: the user moves a camera with keyboard input, and the model generates each subsequent frame conditioned on the action. The result feels like a video game, except the engine is a neural network that has never been told the rules of the world. Genie learns physics, occlusion, perspective, and persistent object identity entirely from pixel sequences.

Imagen 4 World, the world-model variant of Google's Imagen family, takes a related but distinct approach: rather than producing video frames, it produces an editable 3D representation (typically Gaussian splats or a neural radiance field) along with a sparse occupancy grid that other models can query. The pitch is that game engines and robotics simulators can ingest Imagen 4 World outputs directly, sidestepping the conversion step that has historically been the bottleneck. As of mid-2026, both Genie 3 and Imagen 4 World are gated behind partnership programs rather than public APIs, but their existence has reset expectations: when you can prompt for a playable environment, hand-modeled levels start to feel like manual Excel before the introduction of pivot tables.

32.5.4 Synthetic Data for Robots: DreamGen

3D generation is not only for human consumers. NVIDIA's DreamGen, released in 2025, uses video world models to generate synthetic robot trajectories, addressing the chronic data scarcity that limits the Vision-Language-Action models discussed in Section 32.1. The pipeline: a robot demonstrates one task in one environment via teleoperation. DreamGen then uses a world model to synthesize many more trajectories of that robot performing related tasks in different environments. The resulting synthetic demonstrations are used to train the policy, which now generalizes to 22 new tasks across 10 environments from a single seed demonstration. This is sim-to-real, but the sim is generated by a neural network rather than authored in Isaac Sim or Gazebo.

Tip

When evaluating a 3D generation model for production, the gating metric is rarely visual fidelity. It is mesh hygiene: does the output have manifold geometry, sensible UV unwrapping, reasonable polygon count, and clean collision proxies? A photorealistic but non-manifold mesh is useless to a game engine and dangerous in a physics simulator. Tools like Open3D and the validation pipeline in Trellis exist precisely to filter for shipable outputs. Build the validator into your generation loop, not as a post-hoc cleanup.

32.5.5 The Format Question: Meshes, Splats, NeRFs, or Latents?

One under-discussed challenge is that the 3D asset ecosystem is still fragmented across representations. Polygon meshes (OBJ, GLB, USD) are the lingua franca of game engines and DCC software. Gaussian splats are dominant for real-time rendering of captured scenes. Neural radiance fields produce stunning visualizations but are slow to render. Structured latents like Trellis SLAT are convenient for further editing but require model-specific decoders.

In practice, modern generators are converging on a "produce all formats from one inference" pattern, which is genuinely useful but creates a quality ceiling problem: the mesh decoder, the Gaussian decoder, and the radiance field decoder are not equally good, so the format you commit to determines the quality you actually get. Production teams are increasingly choosing the representation first (typically GLB meshes for game integration, splats for cinematic rendering) and selecting their generator based on which decoder is strongest for that format. The OpenUSD standard, championed by NVIDIA and Pixar, is the strongest candidate for a unified format that can carry meshes, splats, and physics annotations in the same file, and most 2025-era generators now ship USD exporters as standard.

What Comes Next

3D asset generation gave us the building blocks of synthetic worlds. Section 32.6 stays within multimodal generation but pivots from creating from scratch to editing what already exists: image inpainting, video remixing, audio remixing, and scene relighting. That constraint, surprisingly, is harder.
