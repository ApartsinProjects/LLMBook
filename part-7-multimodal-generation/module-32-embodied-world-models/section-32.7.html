Multimodal Reasoning & Cross-Modal Retrieval

Section 32.7
Big Picture

Generation produces; reasoning understands. The same multimodal stack that generates images and videos in Chapter 31 can be turned inside out to power retrieval ("find me images like this"), classification ("is this photo of a dog or a fox?"), grounded question answering ("how many people are in this picture?"), and the kind of complex visual reasoning that benchmarks like MMMU were built to measure. This section closes the multimodal arc by tracing how a single architectural idea, contrastive image-text alignment introduced by CLIP, evolved into the dual-purpose embedding-and-reasoning models that ship in every consumer-facing AI product as of 2026. Cross-modal retrieval and multimodal reasoning are the same problem at different temperatures: at low temperature you retrieve the nearest neighbor; at high temperature you generate a chain of thought across modalities.

Prerequisites

This section assumes familiarity with embeddings and vector retrieval from Chapter 22, the vision encoders introduced in Section 31.1, and the evaluation patterns from Chapter 34. A working understanding of contrastive learning helps; the original embedding concept from Section 22.1 is the right starting point for the dual-encoder pattern this section explores.

The intellectual origin of cross-modal retrieval is the observation that an image and its caption describe the same concept from two different angles. If you train a model to map both into a shared embedding space, where the image vector and the caption vector for the same concept land close together, you have built a translator between modalities. That translation is the foundation of every modern multimodal product: image search by text query, text generation conditioned on visual context, zero-shot classification by ranking text labels against an image embedding, and content moderation by comparing image embeddings to a database of disallowed content. Search, retrieval, and reasoning are all downstream of this one alignment.

The story divides into three threads: contrastive encoders (CLIP and descendants) that produce the embeddings, generative VLMs (LLaVA, BLIP-3, GPT-4V) that consume embeddings to answer questions, and the evaluation benchmarks that measure whether either kind of model genuinely understands a scene rather than exploiting shortcuts. All three threads converge in production, where a single deployed system typically uses contrastive embeddings for retrieval and a generative VLM for reasoning, with a shared backbone reducing duplicate inference cost.

32.7.1 The Contrastive Lineage: CLIP, SigLIP, and Beyond

CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) trained image and text encoders jointly with a contrastive loss: given a batch of N image-caption pairs, the model predicts which caption matches which image via a softmax across all N pairs. The result was a model that learned a shared embedding space where image embeddings and their corresponding text embeddings were nearby, while non-matching pairs were pushed apart. Trained on 400 million web image-text pairs, CLIP achieved 76% zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet, matching ResNet-50 without seeing a single ImageNet label during training. That number was the inflection point that made the rest of the field follow.

The descendants iterated on three axes. Loss function: SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) replaced the softmax contrastive loss with a pairwise sigmoid loss, decoupling batch size from loss normalization and allowing efficient training at both large and small scale. Data scale: OpenCLIP and EVA-CLIP pushed training data to billions of pairs, with corresponding quality jumps. Resolution and patch size: SigLIP-2 and the Shape-Optimized 400M variant added dynamic resolution handling, letting the encoder ingest images at native aspect ratios without distortion.

The practical upshot for builders is that you almost never train a CLIP-family encoder from scratch. You pick a pre-trained checkpoint (OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 for most retrieval tasks, SigLIP-2 for production inference where latency matters, EVA-CLIP for top-of-leaderboard accuracy on benchmarks) and either use the embeddings directly or fine-tune the last few layers on your domain. The big_vision codebase from Google Research and the open_clip repository together host most of the publicly available checkpoints.

Key Insight

CLIP embeddings are the multimodal equivalent of text embeddings. The mental model is the same: you have a fixed-dimensional vector for each item, you store these in a vector database, you query with cosine similarity, you retrieve the top K. The only difference is that the items can now be images, video frames, audio clips, or 3D objects, and the queries can be any modality the encoder supports. This is what powers Pinterest's visual search, Google Images "search by image" feature, Spotify's similar-track recommendation, and the moderation pipelines that flag CSAM and other prohibited content. The reasoning models in the next subsection sit on top of this retrieval layer, not in place of it.

32.7.2 Generative VLMs: From LLaVA to BLIP-3

While contrastive encoders are excellent at retrieval, they cannot answer "how many people in this image are wearing hats?" because that requires explicit reasoning that the encoder's pooled vector does not preserve. The generative VLM thread answers this by combining a frozen (or partially fine-tuned) vision encoder with a language model decoder. The encoder converts the image into a sequence of patch embeddings; a lightweight projection layer maps those into the language model's token space; the language model autoregressively generates the answer.

LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) demonstrated that this architecture, trained on roughly 150,000 instruction-following examples generated by GPT-4 from image captions, could match GPT-4V on visual question answering at a fraction of the compute cost. The lineage from LLaVA includes LLaVA-NeXT, LLaVA-OneVision, and a long tail of domain-specific variants (LLaVA-Med for medical imagery, LLaVA-Plus for tool use, mini-CPM-V for on-device inference). The architecture template (vision encoder + projector + LLM decoder) is so dominant that it is essentially the default modern VLM recipe.

BLIP-3 (also released as xGen-MM by Salesforce in 2024) is the most cited open framework in this lineage, releasing 4B and 14B models with full training code, datasets, and recipes. BLIP-3's contribution is less architectural and more methodological: it documents what works at scale across data curation, training stages, and evaluation, and it open-sources the artifacts needed to reproduce results. For teams building their own VLM, BLIP-3 is the closest the open community has to a "production recipe."

32.7.3 Evaluating Multimodal Reasoning: MMMU and the Saturation Problem

Measuring whether a model genuinely reasons about images is harder than measuring whether it can describe them. The 2024 to 2026 generation of benchmarks (MMMU, MathVista, MMVet, RealWorldQA, MMBench) test compositional reasoning, spatial understanding, chart interpretation, and grounded multi-step inference. MMMU in particular spans 30 academic disciplines with 11,500 questions covering subjects from accounting to art theory, with multiple-choice and open-ended formats.

The uncomfortable result is that the top frontier models (Gemini 2.5 Ultra, GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.6) are now within a few points of human expert performance on most multimodal reasoning benchmarks, including MMMU. This is the same saturation pattern that hit text benchmarks two years earlier. The community response has been the same: build harder benchmarks (MMMU-Pro adds 10 multiple-choice distractors per question to defeat single-image classification shortcuts), evaluate on out-of-distribution tasks, and move toward live evaluations where the benchmark itself is private and rotated.

Tip

When productionizing a VLM, do not rely on benchmark scores alone. Build a domain-specific evaluation harness with 100 to 500 hand-curated examples that cover your actual use case, then track regression against that set on every model update. Public benchmarks are necessary but not sufficient; they tell you about general capability, not whether your model can correctly read your specific chart format or identify your specific product SKUs. The evaluation patterns from Chapter 34 apply directly here, with the addition that you should include images at multiple resolutions and aspect ratios to catch encoder-side regressions.

32.7.4 Production Patterns: When to Retrieve, When to Reason

The design choice that separates novice and production multimodal systems is when to use retrieval versus when to use reasoning. A pure VLM call costs cents per image and seconds per inference; a retrieval lookup costs fractions of a cent and milliseconds. For high-volume tasks (visual search, duplicate detection, content moderation, recommendation), retrieval with CLIP-style embeddings is almost always the right primary tool, with VLMs reserved for the slower path of explaining or auditing the retrieval.

A common production pattern combines both: an embedding-based retriever returns the top 20 candidate images, a VLM then re-ranks them by reasoning over the user's intent and the image content. This is the multimodal analog of the retrieve-then-rerank pattern that has been standard in RAG pipelines for years. The cost ratio works out: 20 cheap retrievals plus 1 expensive reasoning call beats 1000 expensive reasoning calls for the same recall at far lower latency.
