Part VII: Multimodal Generation

Vision, audio, video, embodied AI, world models, and the cross-modal reasoning stack that powers 2026 multimodal products.

"Language is just one channel. The 2026 frontier is the model that hears, sees, plans, acts, and explains itself in the same forward pass."

Author's notebook, after Veo 3 / Sora 2 / GPT-5-omni launches, Q4 2025

Part Overview

Part VII lifts the LLM stack off the page. The text-only chapters of Parts I-VI gave you a model that reads and writes; this part gives you a model that also sees, hears, moves, and imagines. Three chapters carry the thread: Chapter 31 covers the generative stack of images, audio, video, and document understanding, then introduces unified omni-architectures (GPT-5-omni, Gemini 2 Pro Vision, Llama 4 Scout) where one model handles all modalities in a single forward pass. Chapter 32 lifts that stack into 3D and into action: vision-language-action models for robotics, world models for predicting futures before acting, 3D Gaussian Splatting as the neural scene representation that ties perception to manipulation, and cross-modal reasoning as the substrate that makes everything controllable. Chapter 33 consolidates the working toolchain: which platforms, libraries, datasets, and models matter in practice for shipping a real multimodal product in 2026.

Real-world LLM applications across industries (legal, finance, healthcare, code, cybersecurity, government, manufacturing, creative, recommendations / search) now live in Part XI; do not look for them here.

Big Picture

The defining shift of 2024-2026 is that "multimodal" stopped meaning "an LLM plus a separate image model glued together with a router." Frontier models now process tokens for text, image patches, audio mel-spectrograms, and video frames in a unified embedding space, then decode whichever modality the task needs. Generation, perception, action, and reasoning collapse into one model. This part walks the seam between three views of that collapse: the generative view (Chapter 31: how do you produce pixels, sound, and video from a prompt), the embodied view (Chapter 32: how do you produce actions in a 3D world from a prompt), and the practitioner view (Chapter 33: what does the working toolchain actually look like in production this quarter).

The unifying technical thesis

Every section in Part VII solves the same problem in a different substrate: How do you align a model's representation with the structure of a non-text modality such that language can steer it? Diffusion models do it with iterative denoising guided by text embeddings. VLA models do it by tokenizing motor commands. World models do it by predicting the next frame as the loss. Gaussian splats do it by parameterizing geometry as differentiable Gaussians a transformer can edit. Once you see the alignment thesis, the three chapters stop looking like three subfields and start looking like one engineering pattern applied to three substrates.

Every modality becomes tokens

The unifying engineering move across this Part is brutally simple: replace BPE text tokens with whatever token vocabulary your modality wants, then run the same transformer. Image patches become tokens (ViT, the basis of Section 31.1 generators and Section 32.7 reasoners). Audio mel-frames become tokens through an EnCodec or SoundStream codec (Section 31.2). Motor commands become discrete bins added to the LM vocabulary (Section 32.1 VLAs). Video frame patches become tokens for diffusion transformers (Section 32.4 world models). The softmax over a vocabulary, the attention math from Section 3.3, the next-token loss from Section 7.2 all transfer unchanged. Gato (Reed et al., 2022, arXiv:2205.06175) and Chameleon (Meta, 2024, arXiv:2405.09818) are the canonical demonstrations that this is not a metaphor.

One loss, four substrates

Next-token prediction (Section 7.2), next-frame prediction (Section 32.4), next-action prediction (Section 32.1), and next-pixel denoising (Section 31.1) all minimize a likelihood under a learned model. The forward pass and the loss surface are structurally identical; only the substrate of "the next thing" changes. When you read the equations in any Part VII section, the maximum-likelihood objective you absorbed in Part II is staring back at you.
