SLOs, Alerting, and FinOps

Section N.5
What this section is

The reference for the two operational disciplines that decide whether your LLM system is in good standing with both users and the finance team: SLOs and alerting for reliability, FinOps for cost. The two interact directly because almost every LLM cost optimization (prompt caching, batch APIs, lower-cost models) is also a latency intervention, and most reliability problems (retries, timeouts, fallback chains) directly drive cost.

The conceptual treatment of scaling economics and post-launch operations lives in Chapter 47 (Scaling Economics) and Chapter 49 (Post-Launch Monitoring). This section is the practitioner reference: which SLOs to define for LLM endpoints, how to wire alerts to PagerDuty or Opsgenie without paging on noise, and which three cost-attribution strategies cover the bulk of the FinOps work. The opening case study is the \$12,000 fintech bill from Appendix T War Story 5; the operational fix touches every concept in this section.

O.5.1 SLOs for LLM Endpoints

A Service Level Objective (SLO) is a target on a Service Level Indicator (SLI) measured over a window. For a stateless REST service the classical SLIs are availability (success-rate), latency (p99), and throughput; for an LLM endpoint you add LLM-specific SLIs that the classical set misses.

Availability: percentage of requests that return a successful response within the timeout. For LLM systems the timeout itself is a design choice (a 30s timeout for a chat completion is reasonable; a 30s timeout for a long-form generation is not). Target a 99.5% to 99.9% monthly SLO depending on user-facing criticality.

Latency, split: separate time-to-first-token (TTFT) from total response time. TTFT is what the user perceives as "the system is alive"; total time is what the user perceives as "the system is done". Set independent SLOs (TTFT p99 < 1s, total p99 < 30s) so a slow long-tail does not hide a TTFT regression. Inter-token latency p99 is a third useful SLI for streaming UIs.

Quality SLO (the LLM-specific addition): hallucination rate, faithfulness score, or refusal rate measured by eval-in-prod (Section N.2). A typical RAG SLO is "Ragas faithfulness p50 >= 0.85, p10 >= 0.70 over a 7-day window". Quality SLOs differ from availability SLOs in that you cannot fix them with a retry; the fix is a model, prompt, or retrieval change.

Cost SLO: per-request or per-user-session cost target. Without a cost SLO, the runaway-bill scenario (War Story 5) is a tail risk that hits you once and is then fixed reactively. With a cost SLO, an alert fires the moment per-session cost crosses a threshold and the kill-switch (P12 pattern from Chapter 35) trips automatically.

Key Insight

Quality and cost are SLIs in their own right for LLM systems, not derived consequences of availability and latency. Treating them as first-class with explicit SLOs is the single biggest operational maturity step beyond classical SRE practice; teams that skip this step end up paging on availability while quality silently degrades or while the bill triples.

O.5.2 Alerting, Error Budgets, and On-Call Wiring

An SLO without an alert is decoration. The standard pattern is to convert the SLO into an error budget (the complement: 0.1% downtime allowance for a 99.9% SLO) and alert on the rate at which the budget is being burned. Google's SRE workbook documents the multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting policy that is now standard. The short form: a fast alert (5-minute window) catches acute outages; a slow alert (hour or day window) catches gradual degradation.

Wire alerts to PagerDuty or Opsgenie via the Prometheus Alertmanager (for self-hosted stacks) or directly from your observability backend (Datadog, Honeycomb, Grafana). The router rules matter: route LLM-specific alerts (quality, cost) to the ML on-call rotation, not the infra rotation, because the playbooks differ. A latency alert is fixed by adding capacity or rolling back a deploy; a quality alert is fixed by rolling back a prompt or model version, which is a different runbook and a different decision authority.

# Prometheus alerting rule: cost SLO with burn-rate alert
groups:
  - name: llm-cost-slo
    rules:
      - alert: PerSessionCostBurnFast
        expr: |
          (rate(llm_session_cost_usd_sum[5m])
           / rate(llm_session_cost_usd_count[5m])) > 0.50
        for: 2m
        labels: {severity: page, team: ml-platform}
        annotations:
          summary: "Avg per-session cost > $0.50 (target < $0.10)"
          runbook: "https://runbooks.company.com/llm/cost-spike"
      - alert: HallucinationRateBurn
        expr: |
          avg_over_time(ragas_faithfulness_p10[1h]) < 0.70
        for: 15m
        labels: {severity: page, team: ml-platform}
        annotations:
          summary: "Faithfulness p10 below 0.70 over 1h (quality SLO breach)"

O.5.3 FinOps: Per-Request Cost Attribution

The FinOps equation has three terms: vendor token (or self-host compute) cost, proxy and gateway cost, downstream cost (storage, network, eval-in-prod judges). The unit-economics question is the marginal cost of one user session, which requires emitting both input and output token counts on every span. The OpenLLMetry semantic conventions (Section N.1) define gen_ai.usage.input_tokens and gen_ai.usage.output_tokens as standard attributes. Multiplying by per-model rate cards (which differ by 10x+ across models) gives per-span cost; aggregating by user_id, session_id, or feature flag gives the per-segment view. Helicone, Langfuse, and Phoenix ship this dashboard out of the box; rolling your own is one Prometheus exporter consuming the trace stream.

O.5.4 The 2024-2026 Cost Optimization Toolkit

Three vendor-side mechanisms cover most LLM cost optimization. Prompt caching (OpenAI, Oct 2024; Anthropic, Aug 2024; Gemini) charges ~10% of the normal input rate for cached tokens. For a RAG app where 90% of every prompt is shared context, this is a 5-10x reduction with no quality change. Batch APIs (OpenAI Batch, Anthropic Message Batches) charge ~50% with a 24-hour SLA, ideal for offline eval and content pipelines. Model routing sends easy queries to a cheaper tier (gpt-4o-mini, claude-haiku-4-5) and escalates only the hard ones; LiteLLM and Not Diamond are the 2024 entrants. For self-hosted serving (Appendix K) the levers are GPU utilization (idle vs busy: same dollars/hour), quantization (FP8 or INT4 weights), and continuous batching (vLLM's PagedAttention, TGI). A GPU at 50% utilization is a 2x cost overage compared to a properly batched one.

Postmortem: The \$12,000 Fintech Bill

The full case study is War Story 5 in Appendix T. The chain of failures: a buggy release produced 429 rate-limit errors at 5% of requests; the retry layer multiplied each failure by 7; the retry resubmitted the full 6K-token conversation history every time; no prompt caching was enabled; no cost SLO existed and no per-session cost alert fired. The \$12K bill arrived in 14 hours, vs an expected \$400. The remediation touched every section in this appendix: prompt caching for the shared prefix (50% input savings), jittered exponential retry with hard cap (P7 pattern), per-request cost attribution emitted on every span (Section N.1), a per-session cost SLO with a fast-burn alert and an automatic kill switch (this section). The lesson is that no single fix would have prevented it; the production-engineering discipline is what would have.

Key Takeaway
  • Define four SLI families for LLM endpoints: availability, latency (split TTFT from total), quality (faithfulness, hallucination rate), and cost (per-request or per-session). Quality and cost are first-class, not afterthoughts.
  • Use multi-window multi-burn-rate alerts wired to PagerDuty or Opsgenie; route LLM-specific alerts (quality, cost) to the ML on-call, not the infra on-call, because the playbooks differ.
  • Emit per-request token counts on every trace span (OpenLLMetry semantic conventions); multiply by rate-card prices to get attributed cost by user, feature, or tenant.
  • Three vendor-side savings levers cover most of the FinOps space: prompt caching (2024 GA on OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini), batch APIs at ~50% rates, and cheaper-model routing via LiteLLM or Not Diamond.
  • The runaway-bill failure mode is real (Appendix T War Story 5); prevent it with a per-session cost SLO, a fast-burn alert, a hard kill switch, and prompt caching for shared prefixes.
