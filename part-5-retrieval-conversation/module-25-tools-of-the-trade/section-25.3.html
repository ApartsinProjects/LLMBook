Datasets & Benchmarks

Section 25.3

RAG datasets fall into three categories: retrieval-quality benchmarks (does my embedding find the right chunk?), end-to-end QA benchmarks (does the system answer correctly?), and the embedding-training corpora behind sentence-transformers and BGE.

25.3.1 Retrieval benchmarks

  • BEIR (UKP Lab, 2021) is a heterogeneous information-retrieval benchmark comprising 18 datasets spanning news, biomedical, finance, and Wikipedia-style retrieval. Its objective is to measure zero-shot retrieval generalization (train on one domain, evaluate on another) so you can tell whether an embedding model overfits to its training distribution, which matters because in-domain leaderboard wins do not transfer. The core concept is normalized scoring (nDCG@10) across diverse domains so a single number summarizes cross-domain robustness. Pick BEIR when comparing embedding models you intend to deploy on novel data; if your domain matches one of its tasks, look at that task specifically rather than the average.
  • MTEB (HuggingFace + community, 2022) is the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark, covering 56 tasks across 7 domains (retrieval, clustering, classification, reranking, STS, summarization, pair classification) and dozens of languages. Its objective is to be the comprehensive embedding leaderboard that captures more than retrieval alone, which matters when your embeddings are reused for clustering or classification downstream. The core concept is a single mean score across heterogeneous tasks; the recent MTEB v2 split out per-domain leaderboards. Pick MTEB to compare embedding models; treat the top-of-leaderboard score skeptically because contamination and overfitting to MTEB tasks are well-documented.
  • MS MARCO (Microsoft, 2016) is the web-passage retrieval dataset built from Bing user queries with human-annotated relevance judgments over 8.8M passages. Its objective is to provide the canonical training set for dense retrieval models, which mattered because every modern dense retriever (DPR, ANCE, ColBERT, E5, BGE) was trained or pretrained on it. The core concept is real queries with annotated relevant passages plus a leaderboard tracking ranking metrics. Pick MS MARCO for training dense retrievers or for sanity-checking that your model generalizes off it; for production eval, BEIR is more rigorous because MS MARCO is in-distribution training data for most embedding models.
  • BEIR-NL, BEIR-PL (2024) are multilingual BEIR variants for Dutch and Polish; relevant for non-English retrieval evaluation.
  • NoLiMa (Modarressi et al., 2024) is a long-context retrieval benchmark that tests whether retrievers actually use the full context window or collapse to the first / last few hundred tokens. The 2024 "Lost in the Middle" follow-ups are the relevant literature; long-context retrieval is broken in subtle ways and benchmarks like NoLiMa surface them.
  • RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) is the leading hallucination-detection-in-RAG benchmark; cite it when you need a public reference for "does this RAG system hallucinate".
  • ReSearch (2025) and SearchBench: newer agentic-retrieval benchmarks that test retrieval-as-tool-use rather than retrieval-as-lookup.

25.3.2 End-to-end RAG / QA benchmarks

  • SQuAD 2.0 (Stanford, 2018) is the Stanford Question Answering Dataset extended with unanswerable questions to test whether a model can abstain. Its objective is extractive QA over Wikipedia paragraphs where the answer is a contiguous span (or no answer at all), which matters as a baseline for closed-book and short-context QA. The core concept is exact-match and F1 scoring against gold spans. Pick SQuAD as a smoke test for span-based QA; for modern RAG eval it is too narrow and saturated.
  • Natural Questions (Google, 2019) is a QA dataset built from real anonymized Google search queries paired with Wikipedia pages and human-annotated short and long answers. Its objective is to evaluate open-domain QA on questions a real user actually asked, which matters because curated benchmark questions over-represent factoid lookup. The core concept is two-tier evaluation: short answer (span or yes/no) and long answer (paragraph). Pick NQ for open-domain RAG eval; the questions skew toward Wikipedia-style factoids, so combine with HotpotQA for reasoning.
  • HotpotQA (Carnegie Mellon, 2018) is a multi-hop QA dataset where answering requires reasoning over two or more Wikipedia paragraphs. Its objective is to stress-test whether a retrieval-and-reasoning pipeline can combine evidence from multiple sources, which matters because single-hop retrieval is too easy to be diagnostic. The core concept is supporting-fact annotation: gold spans across multiple documents the system must find and combine. Pick HotpotQA for testing multi-hop reasoning; note that frontier models have largely saturated it, so look at recent variants like 2WikiMultihopQA and MuSiQue for harder evaluation.
  • MultiSpanQA (Google, 2022) is a question-answering benchmark where answers are multiple discontinuous spans rather than a single span. Its objective is to evaluate the realistic case where an answer is a list ("Which countries border France?") which matters because span-based QA over-trains on the single-fact pattern. Pick MultiSpanQA when your application requires list-style answers; for prose-style RAG output, RAGAS-style eval is more informative.
  • RAGAS (Exploding Gradients, 2023) is an LLM-as-judge framework for evaluating end-to-end RAG output on metrics like faithfulness, answer relevance, context precision, and context recall. Its objective is to give production teams a way to evaluate RAG on their own data without writing custom judge prompts, which matters because every RAG application has bespoke documents that no public benchmark covers. The core concept is decomposing "is this answer good?" into faithfulness (is it grounded in retrieved context?) and relevance (does it answer the question?), each scored by a strong LLM. Pick RAGAS as your production RAG eval default; pair with the academic benchmarks above for cross-team comparison.

25.3.3 Embedding-training corpora

  • SBERT training data (UKP Lab, 2019+) is the collection of (anchor, positive, negative) triplets and (anchor, positive) pairs used to train the canonical sentence-transformer models. Its objective is to provide a curated set of weak-supervision pairs (paraphrases, NLI premise-hypothesis pairs, question-answer pairs) for contrastive embedding training, which matters when you want to train or fine-tune your own embedding model. The core concept is multiple-source contrastive learning where positives come from diverse paraphrase sources to learn a general similarity metric. Pick this when fine-tuning a domain-specific embedding model; for production retrieval, off-the-shelf BGE / E5 are usually strong enough.
  • Hugging Face embedding-training-data (HuggingFace, 2023) is the aggregated mix of pair and triplet datasets that BGE, E5, and many other open embedding models train on. Its objective is to provide a single normalized download for the dozens of upstream sources, which matters when you do not want to write 30 dataset adapters. Pick it as the entry point for embedding pretraining; layer your own in-domain pairs on top for fine-tuning.

25.3.4 Comparing the datasets

25.3.1 RAG benchmarks
Dataset Type Use Caveat
BEIR Retrieval Embedding quality English-only
MTEB Retrieval + clustering Embedding selection Gaming via test-leak
NQ End-to-end QA System quality Older domain
HotpotQA Multi-hop QA Reasoning retrieval Saturated by frontier models
RAGAS Eval framework Custom RAG eval LLM-judge cost
Eval RAG, not just retrieval

BEIR and MTEB measure embedding quality. RAGAS-style end-to-end eval measures whether the final answer is grounded and correct. The two metrics frequently diverge: a system can have great recall and bad answers, or vice versa. Always measure both.
