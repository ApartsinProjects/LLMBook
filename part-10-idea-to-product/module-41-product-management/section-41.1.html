From Hypothesis to Product Spec

Section 41.1

"A spec that does not commit to a latency budget, a cost ceiling, and a failure mode is a wish, not a plan."

A Pragmatic Compass, Specification-Hardening AI Agent
Big Picture

The space between "we have an idea" and "engineering starts coding" is where most LLM projects quietly go off the rails. The hypothesis from Chapter 40 is one paragraph: a user, a capability, a willingness to pay, and a riskiest assumption. The product spec is the contract that turns that paragraph into something three different teams (product, engineering, and the business owner) can agree on without later disputing what was meant. This section gives you the structure of that contract: the must-haves you cannot leave fuzzy, the numbers you have to commit to up front, and the small but critical artifacts (a risk tier, a failure-mode catalogue, an acceptance test) that protect you from the most expensive failure in AI product work, which is shipping a thing that nobody can agree is finished.

Prerequisites

This section builds directly on the ideation hypothesis from Section 40.1. Familiarity with the evaluation patterns from Chapter 34 and the LLM API patterns from Chapter 13 is recommended; the spec talks about numbers (p95 latency, cost per call, quality scores) that come from those chapters.

41.1.1 Why LLM Specs Look Different from Traditional Software Specs

A traditional product spec for a checkout form is mostly about features: what fields, what validation, what error messages, what payment processors. The acceptance criteria are crisp: "click 'Pay,' card is charged, confirmation page loads in under two seconds." If the system meets these criteria, it is done. If it does not, the bug is reproducible and the fix is identifiable.

LLM specs cannot work this way. The system is non-deterministic by construction (see Section 45.1), the cost varies per call, the quality is statistical, and the failure modes are language-shaped rather than exception-shaped. A spec that says "the chatbot answers questions correctly" is meaningless: which questions, how is "correctly" measured, how often is "often enough," and what happens on the cases where the model is wrong? Without committing to specific answers, the team will rediscover the same arguments every two-week sprint review.

Key Insight

An LLM product spec is a contract about distributions, not single outcomes. It commits to ranges, percentiles, and statistical thresholds, not to point estimates. "p95 latency under 3 seconds" is a spec; "fast responses" is not. "Quality score above 85 on the golden eval set" is a spec; "good answers" is not. The discipline of converting feelings into numbers is what separates a spec from a wish list, and it has to happen before code starts.

41.1.2 The Six Sections of an LLM Product Spec

Specs come in many templates and shapes. The minimum useful version, regardless of company size, contains the following six sections. If any one of them is missing, an argument about scope is essentially guaranteed at some point in the next quarter.

  1. User and Job-To-Be-Done. One sentence each. Who is the user? What were they doing before the product existed? What outcome are they trying to reach? Borrowed from the Jobs-To-Be-Done framework popularised by Christensen and Hall, this section forces the team to talk about humans, not features.
  2. Capability and Role. Which of the seven capabilities from Section 40.1.5 is the product exercising? Is the LLM a Drafter, Classifier, Router, Analyst, Agent, or something else? This single decision drives the rest of the spec. The Role Canvas in Section 45.2 is the concrete artifact.
  3. Functional requirements. What inputs does the system accept? What outputs does it produce? What are the supported languages, document types, length limits? Crucially: what is explicitly out of scope? An LLM product that does not say what it will refuse to do will be asked to do everything.
  4. Non-functional requirements (the numbers). The latency budget (p50, p95, max), the cost ceiling (per request, per user, per month), the quality threshold (the score on the golden eval set), and the availability target (uptime, fallback behaviour during model outages). These are the numbers engineering will design against.
  5. Risk tier and failure-mode catalogue. What level of harm is possible if the model is wrong? Who is accountable when it happens? See Section 41.1.4 for the four-tier risk framework. The catalogue is short on purpose: three to five named failure modes and their mitigations.
  6. Acceptance test. A specific, runnable definition of "done." Not "QA approves," but "the system passes the 100-case golden eval at a score above X, and 10 internal pilot users use it for 5 sessions without escalating to a human." Concrete enough that the engineer and the PM cannot disagree about whether the test passed.

The combined length of these six sections, written densely, is usually two to three pages. If your spec is longer, you have leaked design decisions into it; pull them out and put them in the design doc. If your spec is shorter, you are skipping something the team will fight about later.

41.1.3 Risk Tiers: What Happens When the Model Is Wrong

Every LLM product spec needs an explicit risk tier. This is not bureaucratic safety theatre; it determines what infrastructure you have to build before launch. A consumer entertainment chatbot and a medical triage assistant both use the same API, but their guardrails, evaluation budgets, and escalation paths must be wildly different. Committing to a tier in the spec keeps everyone honest.

LLM Product Risk Tiers (as of 2026)
Tier Failure consequence Examples Required guardrails
T1: Inconvenience User regenerates or ignores; no lasting harm Marketing copy drafter, internal note summariser Thumbs-up/down feedback; basic eval suite
T2: Rework Wrong answer triggers manual review or rework Customer support assistant, lead classifier Confidence labels; human-in-loop on low confidence; logged eval
T3: Financial or reputational Wrong answer costs money or harms trust Quote generator, contract reviewer, fraud triage Mandatory human approval; double-check models; immutable audit log
T4: Safety-critical Wrong answer endangers people, money, or compliance Medical triage, financial advice, autonomous actions on funds Regulatory review; formal verification; high-confidence-only outputs; full traceability

The most common spec mistake is assigning a Tier 3 problem to a Tier 1 team. A "harmless little chatbot" that quietly approves discount codes is a financial-tier product whether anyone called it that or not. Tier assignment must be done by the business owner, in writing, with the failure mode named explicitly. If nobody will sign their name to a tier, nobody owns the product, and the launch will stall the moment a real failure happens.

Warning: Don't Let "Just an MVP" Hide a Tier 3 Risk

The phrase "it's just an MVP" is the most reliable way to skip a risk-tier conversation. MVPs are not exempt from the consequences of being wrong; they are exempt from being polished. A medical triage MVP is still a Tier 4 product, even if it ships to ten beta users. Be especially suspicious of phrases like "we'll add guardrails later" or "the user knows it's experimental." Real users do not internalise warning labels (recall the automation complacency research summarised in Section 45.1); they trust the output and act on it.

41.1.4 Latency, Cost, and Quality Budgets

Three numbers belong in the spec before any code is written: a latency budget, a cost ceiling, and a quality threshold. These three drive almost every downstream engineering decision: model choice, prompt complexity, RAG configuration, agent depth, and infrastructure.

Latency budget. Commit to a p95, not an average. Frontier models add hundreds of milliseconds per call; reasoning models can take tens of seconds; agentic workflows compose multiple calls. If the user expects a chat-style response, your p95 must live under about 4 seconds or engagement collapses (see Jeff Dean's tail-latency keynote for the underlying math). If the user expects an async report, you have minutes or hours. The spec must say which.

Cost ceiling. Two numbers: cost per successful interaction, and cost per active user per month. The first drives model choice and prompt engineering; the second drives pricing and unit economics. A useful sanity check: if your product's gross margin would not survive a 2x increase in API prices (which has historically happened on a roughly 18-month cycle, in either direction), the architecture is too API-dependent.

Quality threshold. The minimum score on the golden eval set required to ship, and the maximum allowed regression on any subsequent change. The threshold should be assigned before testing, not after, to prevent the universal human tendency to rationalise away poor results. Eugene Yan's writeup on evals is a useful concrete companion.

Real-World Scenario: The Budget That Was Never Written Down

Who: A product team at a B2B SaaS company building an AI email triage feature.

Situation: The hypothesis was strong: classify incoming customer emails into "billing," "technical," and "sales" buckets so the right team got them first. The team had two weeks to ship an MVP.

Problem: No latency budget, no cost ceiling, no quality threshold were written down. Engineering picked the most capable model "to be safe." The result: classification quality was excellent (94%) but each email cost 8 cents to triage. The product became unprofitable above 50 emails per customer per day, which was exactly the segment they wanted to serve.

Fix: The team retrofitted the missing budgets: p95 under 1 second, cost under 0.5 cents per email, quality above 88 percent. They then swapped to a smaller model and added few-shot prompting, landing at 91% quality and 0.3 cents per email. Worse on the leaderboard, dramatically better as a product.

Lesson: The budgets force the team to choose. Without them, the team always picks "the best," and "the best" almost never matches the business case.

41.1.5 The Cross-Functional Spec Review

An LLM product spec only earns its keep when it has been reviewed by the three people who will fight over it later. Skip the review and you will reconstruct the same arguments under deadline pressure six weeks from now.

  • The engineering lead reviews the non-functional requirements. Are the latency, cost, and quality budgets achievable with current models? Where is the riskiest engineering work? What dependencies (eval suite, observability, fallback paths) must be in place before code starts?
  • The business owner reviews the risk tier and the failure-mode catalogue. Do they agree the tier is correct? Have they signed their name to the named failure modes? Is the willingness-to-pay analysis from ideation still credible at the spec'd cost-per-interaction?
  • The user advocate (a designer, a customer-success lead, or a real user) reviews the user and job-to-be-done section. Does the spec describe a problem the user actually has, in their words, or a problem the team wishes they had? This is where most "elegant" solutions die.

The review is short. Thirty minutes, three sign-offs, a shared document with comments resolved. If any of the three reviewers cannot sign, the spec is not done. The most common reason a spec stalls at review is that the team has not actually decided what the product is; the second most common is that the willingness-to-pay analysis from ideation does not survive contact with the cost ceiling.

Tip: Version the Spec, Not Just the Code

The spec changes. The user persona shifts, the cost ceiling moves when the model provider changes prices, the quality threshold tightens as users get more demanding. Track the spec in version control alongside the code, with dated revisions and a one-line changelog ("v0.3: dropped Spanish from MVP scope, added p95 to 2.5s"). When a year later someone asks "why does the product behave this way," the spec history is the answer.
