Building the MVP

Section 44.1

"A prototype that works on your laptop is a hypothesis. An MVP that works for ten real users is evidence."

A Pragmatic Compass, Evidence-Hungry AI Agent
Big Picture

An MVP is the smallest piece of working product that lets real users prove or disprove your hypothesis. For LLM products, the "smallest" part is harder than it sounds: the model is so capable that the team is constantly tempted to add one more feature, one more language, one more edge case. The art of MVP building is making ruthless 80/20 cuts so that you ship something thin but real, then watch what users actually do with it. Most products fail not because the team built the wrong thing, but because they built too much of it before learning whether anyone wanted it. This section gives you the vertical-slice pattern that keeps the scope honest, the internal-pilot mechanics that turn a demo into evidence, and the explicit triggers that tell you whether to keep building, refactor, or pivot.

Prerequisites

This section builds on the product spec from Section 41.1 and the prototype-to-MVP graduation checklist from Section 45.7. Familiarity with the evaluation infrastructure from Chapter 34 is essential; an MVP without evaluation is a demo with a calendar invite.

44.1.1 The 80/20 Cuts: What an LLM MVP Is Allowed to Skip

The MVP question is not "what should we build?" It is "what can we leave out and still test the hypothesis?" For LLM products, the candidates for the chopping block are surprisingly consistent across teams. The following list is what mature LLM teams cut from version one, with a clean conscience, every time.

  • All languages but one. If your hypothesis is "users will pay for an AI legal assistant," prove it in English first. Add Spanish, French, and German after one customer has paid. Each additional language is a multiplier on your eval suite, support burden, and quality regressions.
  • All input formats but the most common one. Accept PDFs in v1; defer DOCX, RTF, scanned images, and email forwards. The 80/20 rule on document types is closer to 95/5: a few formats cover almost all real inputs.
  • All user personas but the riskiest one. Pick the persona whose willingness to pay is most uncertain and build for them alone. If they do not adopt, no other persona will save you. If they do, the other personas often arrive on their own.
  • Auto-scaling, multi-region, high availability. Run your MVP on a single region with manual scaling. Add resilience after you have proven anyone uses it. The Azure Well-Architected guidance and equivalent AWS Well-Architected framework are roadmaps for after product-market fit, not before.
  • Self-service onboarding. Onboard your first 10 users by hand, over video calls. Watch what confuses them. Self-service signup, billing, and quota management are all premature optimisations until you have evidence the product is wanted.
  • Polished UX. A boring text box, a "Run" button, and a result pane is enough to test most hypotheses. The visual design improves once the hypothesis is validated. The Shape Up methodology from Basecamp calls this "drawing fat marker sketches": just enough fidelity to think, no more.
Key Insight

What you cannot cut from an LLM MVP is the evaluation suite, the safety guardrails appropriate to your risk tier, and the basic observability that tells you what users are actually doing. These are not features; they are the instruments that let the MVP do its job, which is to produce evidence. An MVP that ships without them is a demo, not an MVP, and the team will be flying blind the moment the first real user logs in.

44.1.2 The Vertical-Slice Pattern

The most common MVP failure is horizontal scope: the team builds 50 percent of every layer (data ingestion, retrieval, model orchestration, UI, authentication) and ships none of them end-to-end. The vertical-slice pattern is the antidote.

A vertical slice is the thinnest possible end-to-end path through the system that delivers user value. For an LLM product, it usually looks like this:

  1. One input type (say, a pasted block of text).
  2. One LLM capability (one of the seven from Section 40.1.5).
  3. One output format (say, a structured JSON object or a markdown summary).
  4. One user-visible surface (a simple web page, a Slack command, or a CLI).
  5. One feedback signal (thumbs-up/down, "this was useful" button, or a follow-up email).

Each layer in this list is as thin as possible. The point is not to be cheap; it is to be measurable. When something breaks, you know exactly which layer to look at. When the user complains, you know exactly which step in the slice produced the complaint. When the hypothesis fails, you have an unambiguous reading because there is only one path through the system.

Real-World Scenario: The Slice That Saved a Quarter

Who: A four-person team building an AI assistant for tax preparers at a mid-sized accounting firm.

Situation: The original plan called for OCR on scanned receipts, multi-document reasoning across full tax filings, integration with three accounting platforms, multi-tenant authentication, and a polished React UI. Estimated build time: ten weeks.

The vertical slice: A single web form where a preparer pastes the text from one client email and gets back a structured summary of "what the client is asking" plus "what documents we'll need." No OCR, no integration, no authentication beyond a shared link.

Result: Built in five days. The team learned in week two that preparers actually wanted the opposite output: "what should I reply to the client" rather than "what documents do I need." The original spec, with ten weeks of work, would have built the wrong product. The vertical slice cost five days and prevented the wrong-product disaster.

Lesson: The vertical slice's job is to find out what the product should be, not to be the product. Cheap is the feature.

44.1.3 The Internal Pilot Before the Public Launch

Once the vertical slice runs end-to-end, the next step is not the App Store. It is an internal pilot: a small, hand-picked group of real users who agree to use the MVP in real situations, in exchange for direct contact with the team and forgiveness of rough edges. The internal pilot is where the data needed to validate (or kill) the hypothesis actually shows up.

The mechanics matter. A casual "play with it and let me know what you think" generates almost no useful evidence. A structured internal pilot does. The following template, distilled from Google Ventures' design sprint methodology and adapted for LLM products, has produced reliably useful results in dozens of organisations:

  • Pick 5 to 10 users. Fewer and the data is anecdotal; more and you cannot watch each one closely. Choose users whose willingness to pay is real, not friends who will be polite.
  • Set a fixed pilot window. Two to four weeks. Long enough for real usage patterns to emerge, short enough that everyone treats it as urgent.
  • Define one success metric in advance. Pilot users complete the target task using the MVP, without escalating to email or Slack, in at least 60 percent of attempts. Pick your own number, but pick it before the pilot, not after.
  • Instrument every interaction. Log inputs, outputs, latencies, costs, feedback signals, and any user-initiated edits. The observability infrastructure from Chapter 34 is the foundation here. If you can only watch one signal, watch the abandonment rate; it is the single most predictive metric in early-stage AI products.
  • Interview at the midpoint. Talk to each pilot user once around the half-way mark. Listen for what they actually do with the product (not what they say they want). Look for stories of moments when the product surprised them, in either direction.
Tip: The "Wizard of Oz" Internal Pilot

When the riskiest part of the hypothesis is whether users want the output at all (rather than whether the LLM can produce it), consider a Wizard-of-Oz pilot: a human secretly generates the responses, mimicking what the LLM would do. If users do not value the output even when a smart human produces it, the LLM was never going to save the product. This trick has saved more LLM teams from building the wrong thing than any amount of fine-tuning, and it can be set up in a day. See Nielsen Norman Group on Wizard-of-Oz prototyping for a deeper treatment.

44.1.4 When to Keep Building vs. Pivot vs. Kill

At the end of the internal pilot, three signals tell you what to do next. The signals are usually unambiguous once you have agreed on what to look for in advance.

Decision Triggers After an LLM MVP Internal Pilot (as of 2026)
Signal Interpretation Recommended action
Users return unprompted, ask for more features, share with colleagues Hypothesis validated; the product solves a real problem Keep building. Expand scope incrementally. Hold the line on quality and cost.
Users complete the target task but only when reminded; abandonment rate stays high The capability works but the use case is borderline Pivot inside the same hypothesis. Change the user persona, the trigger moment, or the output format. Re-run the pilot.
Users do not return; pilot interviews reveal indifference or confusion The hypothesis is wrong Kill or pivot to a different hypothesis. Do not iterate on a product no one wanted; iterate on the next idea.
Users love the output but the MVP's cost-per-interaction exceeds the willingness-to-pay The product works; the business model does not Re-engineer for cost, then re-pilot. Smaller model, fewer agent steps, semantic caching. Validate that quality survives the cuts.

The hardest of these is the second row. A product that almost works is the single biggest time-sink in LLM product development. The temptation to add "just one more feature" is irresistible. Resist it: the right response to a borderline pilot is usually a sharper repositioning, not more features. The "strategy is about choices" tradition applies here in spades.

Warning: The Sunk-Cost MVP

Once a team has invested four weeks in an MVP, the impulse to "make it work somehow" is overwhelming. Pre-commit to the kill criterion before the pilot starts, in writing, signed by the business owner. If the criterion is met, kill the project even if engineering is mid-stride on the next feature. The cost of killing a four-week MVP is small; the cost of a one-year zombie product that nobody loves is enormous. Andy Grove's famous question from High Output Management, "if I started today, would I do this again?", should be asked weekly during the pilot, not annually at the end.

44.1.5 A 30-Day MVP Plan

The following 30-day plan is the densest, most opinionated version of the framework above. It is not a prescription, but it is what the most disciplined LLM product teams actually do, with minor variations. If your MVP takes much longer than this, the scope is probably too large; the right reaction is not to extend the schedule, but to cut features until 30 days is enough.

  • Days 1 to 3. Lock the spec from Section 41.1. Pick the vertical slice. Identify the riskiest assumption. Get sign-off from engineering, business, and user advocate.
  • Days 4 to 10. Build the vertical slice end-to-end. One LLM capability, one input format, one output format. Wire up evaluation and observability from the start; if you defer them, you will never add them.
  • Days 11 to 14. Internal eval pass. Run the slice against the golden eval set. Tune prompts and configurations until the quality threshold from the spec is met. If you cannot meet it, this is the moment to escalate, not week four.
  • Days 15 to 28. Internal pilot with 5 to 10 real users. Watch every interaction. Interview at the midpoint. Be present in Slack daily. The pilot is the most expensive and most informative phase; do not waste it.
  • Days 29 to 30. Decision day. Review the signals against the decision triggers. Keep, pivot, or kill. Document the decision and the evidence in a one-page write-up that anyone in the organisation could read in five minutes.

This timeline is aggressive but achievable, especially given that a competent team can stand up an LLM prototype in a single afternoon using the patterns from Section 43.1. The hard part is the discipline, not the engineering: holding scope steady, watching pilot users daily, and being willing to kill the project on day 30 if the evidence says so.
