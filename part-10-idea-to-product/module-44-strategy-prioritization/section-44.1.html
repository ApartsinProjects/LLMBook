LLM Strategy & Use Case Prioritization

Section 44.1

The most common reason an LLM project fails is not bad engineering. It is bad strategy: the team picked a use case where the model adds little value, or where the data does not exist, or where the human workflow it tries to replace is the wrong target. Gartner's 2025 surveys put the failure rate of enterprise generative-AI projects at over 60%; BCG's 2025 review finds the dominant pattern is "POC succeeds, scaling fails". Strategy is what separates the 40% that ship from the 60% that don't.

This chapter is the strategy chapter. It opens with what to evaluate before you start, walks through the build-vs-buy decision, builds the business case, and ends with portfolio management once you have multiple projects competing for limited engineering attention. This section sets the framing; Sections 42.2 through 42.4 work through the specific decisions.

44.1.1 Why strategy beats technology in 2026

Every closed-API model in Part III is good enough to power most enterprise use cases. The technical frontier matters for a handful of capabilities (long-context reasoning, agentic computer use, code generation at scale) but for the median "summarize, classify, extract, generate" workload, the model is no longer the constraint. McKinsey's 2025 AI value-realization report finds that the projects that succeed have one trait in common: a clear, measurable, business-aligned objective set before any model was chosen. The projects that fail almost universally chose the model first.

44.1.2 The four readiness pillars

Before selecting a use case, an organization should honestly assess itself across four pillars. The framework below is adapted from the Stanford AI Index's enterprise maturity surveys and the EU's Trustworthy AI assessment criteria:

  • Data maturity: is there a single source of truth for the data your LLM project would touch? Score 1 if siloed, 5 if a documented data mesh with quality SLAs.
  • Technical infrastructure: is there a deployment pipeline ready to host the LLM service? Score 1 if manual deploys, 5 if CI/CD with automated rollback.
  • Organizational culture: do the people who will use the model trust it enough to act on its outputs? Score 1 if "we tried a chatbot once", 5 if AI literacy is part of the onboarding curriculum.
  • Talent: do you have at least one engineer who has shipped an LLM product before, plus enough domain experts to specify the data and evaluation? Score 1 if no, 5 if a cross-functional team is in place.

An honest score below 3 on any pillar means start with a pilot, not a flagship.

Strategy without execution is hallucination

Plenty of organizations announce ambitious AI strategies and never ship anything. The strategy chapter's value is precisely the opposite of that: it forces a connection between "the use cases we picked" and "the production deployments we will be able to support six months from now". A strategy document that does not lead to a deployment plan is worse than no strategy at all, because it consumes leadership attention that could have been spent on a shippable smaller project.

44.1.4 The portfolio view

Almost every organization that ships LLM products at scale runs a portfolio, not a single project. The portfolio mix typically looks like: 60-70% augmentation projects (helping existing employees do their job better), 20-30% process-automation projects (replacing routine work end-to-end), and 5-10% transformative bets (new product surfaces, new business models). MIT Sloan's portfolio research shows this distribution correlates strongly with sustained ROI; portfolios skewed too far toward transformative bets fail expensively, portfolios skewed too far toward augmentation never produce a flagship.

Practical example: the "kill a thing" rule

Healthy AI portfolios kill projects regularly. The pattern that works: every quarter, every project in the portfolio is reviewed against three questions. Does the business case still hold? Is the model still the bottleneck (vs data, process, or trust)? Could the engineering team's time be better spent on a different project? Projects that fail two of three should be paused; projects that fail all three should be killed. The hardest part is killing projects that have visibility but not value; portfolios that cannot do this accumulate zombie projects.

Warning: do not measure projects by demo quality

Demos are easy in 2026. Working in production at the cost-quality-latency point your users will tolerate is hard. The metric that matters at strategy review is not "did the demo wow the stakeholders" but "what is the production-equivalent metric on real workloads against the baseline you specified before starting". If you cannot answer that question, the project is in a pre-strategy state and should not be allocated more resources.
