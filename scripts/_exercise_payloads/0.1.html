Exercise 0.1.1: Loss Function Selection Conceptual

For each scenario, name the loss function you would use and justify why the alternatives are wrong: (a) predicting house price in dollars; (b) predicting whether an email is spam; (c) predicting which of 1000 ImageNet classes an image belongs to; (d) ranking ten search results by relevance.

Answer Sketch

(a) Mean squared error or MAE. MSE penalizes large errors quadratically, suitable when residuals are roughly Gaussian; MAE is more robust to outliers (luxury homes). Cross-entropy is wrong because the target is continuous. (b) Binary cross-entropy on a sigmoid output. MSE on a sigmoid is wrong because it produces vanishing gradients near 0/1 and the wrong probability calibration. (c) Categorical cross-entropy with softmax. Hinge loss works but underperforms for large class counts. MSE on one-hot targets is the classic mistake; the gradient signal is unhealthy and the predicted "probabilities" do not sum to 1. (d) Pairwise ranking loss (RankNet) or listwise (ListNet, LambdaRank). Cross-entropy on relevance grades treats the problem as classification and ignores the ordering constraint that matters for NDCG. The general principle from Section 0.1.3: the loss must reflect what success looks like in your evaluation metric.

Exercise 0.1.2: Bias-Variance in Practice Failure Mode

You train a model and observe: training accuracy 98%, validation accuracy 72%. (a) Diagnose the failure mode. (b) Name three concrete interventions that should help and one that would not. (c) The "Common Misconception" callout notes deep networks can violate the classical bias-variance tradeoff. Explain how a 1B-parameter LLM can fit billions of training tokens and still generalize.

Answer Sketch

(a) Classic high-variance overfitting: the model memorized training but failed to generalize. (b) Helpful: (i) more training data, (ii) regularization (L2, dropout, early stopping), (iii) reduce model capacity (smaller hidden size or fewer layers). Not helpful: training longer, which would make the gap worse. (c) Modern deep networks operate in the "double descent" regime (Belkin 2019, Nakkiran 2020): once a model is sufficiently overparameterized, the implicit bias of SGD plus weight decay finds a flat minimum among the many zero-training-loss solutions, and flat minima generalize. So the classical U-shaped test-error curve gives way to a second descent: very large models continue improving past the interpolation threshold. This is why a 1B-parameter LLM trained on 1T tokens generalizes despite having more parameters than would fit the data in any classical sense.

Exercise 0.1.3: Dropout Math at Inference Calculation

You train a network with dropout p=0.5 on a hidden layer of 1000 units. (a) On average, how many units are active during a single training step? (b) The standard "inverted dropout" implementation scales the surviving activations by 1/(1-p) at training time. Why? What goes wrong if you forget this and instead scale at test time? (c) Why is dropout less commonly used in modern LLM transformers than in 2015-era CNNs?

Answer Sketch

(a) ~500 units, since each unit independently survives with probability 0.5. (b) Without scaling, the expected sum of activations during training is half of the test-time expected sum, so the next layer sees a different distribution at train and test time. Inverted dropout multiplies surviving activations by 1/(1-p) = 2 during training, keeping the expected sum constant. If you forget this and instead scale by 0.5 at test time, you must remember to do so on every inference path including ONNX export, mobile deployment, and quantized inference, which is error-prone. (c) Modern LLMs are usually trained for one or fewer epochs over web-scale data, so overfitting is not the binding constraint. Dropout adds noise that hurts the gradient signal at the scale where every step matters; weight decay and learned positional/token diversity are sufficient regularization. GPT-3 and Llama use dropout=0 in attention and FFN layers.

Exercise 0.1.4: K-Fold When the Data Has Structure Failure Mode

The K-fold cross-validation section warns that random splits can leak information. For each scenario, predict whether random K-fold gives a misleadingly high estimate and propose a correct splitting strategy: (a) time-series stock prices; (b) medical images where one patient may have multiple scans; (c) social network node classification.

Answer Sketch

(a) Random K-fold leaks future information into the training fold (the model "sees" days after the test day in nearby training samples, which is impossible at deployment). Use forward-chaining splits: train on [0, t], test on [t+1, t+k], slide forward. (b) Random splits put scans from the same patient in both train and test, so the model can match patient-specific anatomy rather than learn the disease signal. Use group K-fold with patient ID as the group, ensuring all of patient X's data is in one fold. (c) Random node sampling leaks neighbor labels through message passing. Use temporal or community-based splits, or transductive evaluation that masks test labels but allows graph structure. The general principle: cross-validation is only honest when the splitting respects whatever leakage channel exists in the data; the "Production Alternative" callout discusses the related problem at deployment scale.
