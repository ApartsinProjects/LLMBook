Exercise 1.2.1: BoW Sparsity Math Calculation

You build a bag-of-words representation of a corpus of 50,000 product reviews. The vocabulary after preprocessing has 80,000 unique tokens, and the average review is 60 tokens long. (a) What is the shape of the document-term matrix in dense form, and roughly how much memory does it take in float32? (b) What fraction of entries are non-zero on average? (c) What single change to the representation reduces memory by ~99% with no information loss?

Answer Sketch

(a) Dense shape is 50,000 x 80,000 = 4e9 entries, roughly 16 GB at 4 bytes each. (b) Average non-zero count per row is bounded by the number of unique tokens in a 60-token document, certainly less than 60. So density is at most 60/80,000 = 0.075%. (c) Sparse storage (CSR, COO, or scipy.sparse.csr_matrix) keeps only the non-zero entries plus row/column indices. With ~50 non-zeros per row times 50,000 rows times ~12 bytes per entry, you store ~30 MB instead of 16 GB, a ~500x reduction with no information loss. This is why every classical text pipeline uses sparse matrices and why scikit-learn's CountVectorizer returns one by default.

Exercise 1.2.2: TF-IDF by Hand Calculation

You have a tiny corpus of three documents. The word "transformer" appears in document 1 (twice), document 2 (zero times), and document 3 (once). Compute the IDF for "transformer" using the standard formula $\log(N/\text{df})$ with $N=3$. Then compute the TF-IDF for "transformer" in document 1 using raw term frequency. Now repeat for the word "the", which appears 10 times in every document. What single insight does the comparison demonstrate?

Answer Sketch

For "transformer": df = 2, IDF = log(3/2) approx 0.405. TF in doc 1 = 2, so TF-IDF = 2 * 0.405 = 0.81. For "the": df = 3, IDF = log(3/3) = 0, so TF-IDF = 0 in every document regardless of how often it appears. The insight: IDF kills truly ubiquitous terms even when they have huge raw counts, which is why TF-IDF works as a stop-word-free weighting scheme without an explicit stop list. Note that scikit-learn uses the smoothed variant $\log((1+N)/(1+\text{df})) + 1$, which gives non-zero weight even to terms in every document; the principle is the same but the floor differs.

Exercise 1.2.3: Aggressive Preprocessing Backfires Failure Mode

The "Aggressive Preprocessing Breaks a Sentiment Classifier" callout describes a team that lowercased, stemmed, removed stop words, and stripped punctuation, then watched accuracy drop. For each of the four operations, identify one specific signal that could have been the load-bearing one for sentiment classification.

Answer Sketch

(1) Lowercasing destroys the SHOUTING signal that strongly correlates with negative sentiment in product reviews ("This is GARBAGE!"). (2) Stemming conflates "interest", "interesting", and "interested" into one token, losing the part-of-speech distinction that disambiguates "interesting product" (positive) from "I have an interest" (neutral). (3) Stop-word removal drops "not", "no", "never", which flips polarity; "not bad" becomes "bad". (4) Punctuation stripping deletes "!", "!!!", and "?", which carry intensity and uncertainty signals. The general lesson: every preprocessing step is lossy compression, and the bits you discard may be exactly the ones your downstream task needs. Modern tokenizers preserve case, punctuation, and morphology by default for this reason.

Exercise 1.2.4: Bigram Vocabulary Explosion Code Tweak

You switch your CountVectorizer from ngram_range=(1,1) to ngram_range=(1,2). (a) Estimate how the vocabulary size scales for a corpus where unigram vocabulary is V. (b) Why is the actual bigram vocabulary much smaller than the theoretical $V^2$? (c) Name two practical hyperparameters in CountVectorizer that you would tune to keep memory bounded, and give a sensible starting value for each.

Answer Sketch

(a) Theoretical worst case is V plus $V^2$, dominated by the bigram term, so ~$V^2$. For V = 80,000, that is 6.4 billion possible bigrams. (b) The empirical bigram vocabulary is far smaller because language is Zipfian; almost all word pairs never co-occur. In practice, a 50k-document corpus produces millions, not billions, of distinct bigrams. (c) min_df=5 drops bigrams seen in fewer than five documents, eliminating the long noisy tail; max_features=200000 caps the vocabulary at the most frequent N entries. A common starting recipe is ngram_range=(1,2), min_df=3, max_df=0.95, max_features=200_000. The pattern generalises: the bigram tail is mostly typos and rare proper nouns, neither of which helps a classifier generalise.
