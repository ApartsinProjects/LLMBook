Exercise 1.3.1: Distributional Hypothesis Probe Conceptual

Section 1.3 grounds word2vec in the distributional hypothesis: words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. (a) Construct one minimal-pair example where two words clearly mean different things but the hypothesis would conflate them based on local context. (b) Why does this not catastrophically break word2vec on real corpora? (c) Which downstream task is most affected by this conflation, and which is least?

Answer Sketch

(a) Antonym pair: "good" and "bad" both occur in the context "the movie was ___", "I think the food is ___", "this is a ___ idea". Local distributional context gives them near-identical neighbours, so word2vec assigns them nearby vectors, which is exactly the failure mode you can verify with cosine similarity. (b) On large corpora the wider context distributions diverge enough (negation, intensifiers, downstream effect words) that the vectors are merely close rather than identical. (c) Most affected: sentiment analysis, where antonyms must be cleanly separated. Least affected: topic classification, where "good movie" and "bad movie" are both about movies. The general remedy is contextual embeddings (Section 1.4) which condition on the full sentence, or supervised fine-tuning that pulls antonyms apart.

Exercise 1.3.2: Negative Sampling Math Calculation

The "Why Negative Sampling Matters: The Numbers" callout argues that negative sampling makes word2vec tractable. Suppose your vocabulary is V = 100,000 and you train on 1 billion (center, context) pairs. (a) How many softmax computations does full softmax require per epoch? (b) How many does negative sampling with k=5 negatives require per epoch? (c) What is the speedup factor, and what accuracy cost do you typically pay (qualitatively)?

Answer Sketch

(a) Full softmax requires V dot products per pair to compute the partition function: 1e9 * 1e5 = 1e14 dot products per epoch. Even at modern hardware throughput, this is days to weeks. (b) Negative sampling computes only k+1 = 6 dot products per pair: 1e9 * 6 = 6e9 dot products per epoch. (c) Speedup is roughly 1e14 / 6e9 ~ 17,000x. The accuracy cost is small: Mikolov 2013 reports that k between 5 and 20 reaches similar analogy accuracy as hierarchical softmax. The intuition is that you do not need to push down on all 99,999 wrong words, just on a noise-distribution-weighted sample of them; the gradient signal averages out across millions of training pairs.

Exercise 1.3.3: FastText for OOV Code Tweak

The FastText vignette describes a multilingual support-ticket system. Suppose your word2vec model has no vector for "configurable" but has vectors for "config", "configure", "configured". (a) Sketch how FastText would produce a vector for "configurable" using character n-grams. (b) Why does this fail more gracefully than backing off to an UNK token? (c) Construct one concrete English misspelling for which FastText would still produce a useful vector and explain why.

Answer Sketch

(a) FastText represents each word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors plus the word vector itself. For "configurable" with n in [3,6]: extract n-grams like "<co", "con", "onf", "nfi", "fig", ..., "ble>", look up each in the trained subword embedding table, and sum. The result lives near "configure" and "configured" in the embedding space because the n-gram overlap is high. (b) UNK collapses every unseen word into one vector, so "configurable", "xkqzpd", and "Bismarck" all look identical to the downstream model. FastText preserves morphological similarity. (c) For "configurabel" (misspelled), the n-grams "con", "onf", "nfi", "fig", ..., "abe", "bel" still overlap heavily with the correctly spelled word's n-grams, so cosine similarity between the two vectors stays around 0.9. This is why FastText is the historical default for noisy-text classification.

Exercise 1.3.4: Word Analogy Failure Modes Failure Mode

The classic analogy king - man + woman ~ queen works on the Google News word2vec vectors. The "Word2Vec Does Not Understand Meaning" callout warns this is fragile. (a) Name two analogy categories where word2vec systematically fails. (b) The Levy and Goldberg (2014) paper showed word2vec is implicit matrix factorization. What does that imply about why analogies work at all? (c) Predict whether GloVe (which factorizes co-occurrence directly) would succeed or fail on the same hard categories, and why.

Answer Sketch

(a) Systematically failing categories: (i) commonsense relations like "Paris is the capital of France" only work when both pairs are well-attested; rare countries fail. (ii) Antonym analogies ("hot - cold + happy = ?") rarely yield "sad" because antonyms have similar contexts. (b) Levy and Goldberg show skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI matrix. Analogies work because PMI of co-occurring word pairs encodes consistent linear relationships when the relation is frequent (gender, tense, capital-of), and matrix factorization preserves linear structure. (c) GloVe behaves similarly: it explicitly fits log-co-occurrence ratios, which expose the same linear regularities. So GloVe wins on the easy analogies and fails on the same hard ones; the limitation is the data, not the algorithm. This finding motivated the move to contextual models that condition on the full sentence.
