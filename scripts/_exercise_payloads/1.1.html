Exercise 1.1.1: Place the Era Conceptual

Section 1.1 walks the reader up the representation ladder: rules, statistics, embeddings, contextual embeddings, transformers, LLMs. Place each of the following systems on this ladder and justify in one sentence: (a) ELIZA's psychotherapist script, (b) IBM's Model 1 statistical machine translator, (c) the original word2vec Google News vectors, (d) BERT-base used as a feature extractor, (e) ChatGPT answering a free-form question.

Answer Sketch

(a) Rules era: ELIZA pattern-matches with hand-written templates and has no statistical model at all. (b) Statistical era: Model 1 estimates word-alignment probabilities from parallel corpora but treats words as discrete IDs. (c) Static embeddings: word2vec assigns one dense vector per word type, ignoring context. (d) Contextual embeddings: BERT yields a different vector for the same word in a different sentence, but you still consume it as features. (e) LLM era: ChatGPT is a generative decoder fine-tuned with RLHF to produce free-form responses, replacing the entire feature-plus-classifier stack. The point of the ladder is that each rung subsumes the previous one as a degenerate case rather than discarding it.

Exercise 1.1.2: Predict What Breaks the Pipeline Predictive

The healthcare-startup vignette describes a rule-based triage system that worked until the company opened a new clinic. Without re-reading the section, predict three concrete inputs that would have caused the rules to fail at the new clinic, and explain why a statistical or learned model would handle them better.

Answer Sketch

Likely failures: (1) Regional medical slang or vernacular not in the rule lexicon (a Boston patient says "wicked sore throat", the new Atlanta clinic gets "feel right poorly"). (2) Misspellings and typos that exact-match rules cannot tolerate ("chst pain" instead of "chest pain"). (3) New abbreviations and brand-new drug names that no one wrote a rule for. A statistical model trained on user logs generalises to unseen surface forms because it operates on token frequencies and embeddings rather than literal strings; a contextual model goes further by inferring meaning from neighbouring words, so "killer headache" maps to severity rather than triggering a homicide-keyword rule. This is exactly the brittleness that motivated the field's move from rules to statistics.

Exercise 1.1.3: One Model Replaces Five Failure Mode

The fintech vignette claims a single LLM replaced five task-specific models (intent classification, NER, sentiment, summarization, language detection). Name two reasons this consolidation can be operationally worse despite being engineering-elegant, and one mitigation for each.

Answer Sketch

(1) Latency and cost amplification: a single LLM call typically costs 10-100x more than a small classifier and runs at 100-500 ms instead of single-digit ms. Mitigation: route the high-volume tail of trivial inputs (language detection, simple intent) to a tiny distilled classifier and reserve the LLM for the hard tail. (2) Coupled failure surface: a regression in one prompt-tuned skill (e.g., a refusal pattern that fires on benign inputs) can knock out all five workflows at once. Mitigation: maintain per-skill regression evals and pin the model snapshot, so quality degradations are caught before they cascade. The lesson is that consolidation buys feature velocity but concentrates operational risk; production systems often run a hybrid stack rather than a single monolith.

Exercise 1.1.4: The Discrete-Infinity Argument Conceptual

The "Language as Discrete Infinity" callout argues that natural language combines a finite vocabulary with unbounded composition. (a) Why does this property motivate subword tokenization rather than a fixed word-level vocabulary? (b) What does it imply about the long-tail distribution of n-grams that you will never see in any training corpus? (c) How does this connect to why language models still hallucinate fluently?

Answer Sketch

(a) Word-level vocabularies cannot enumerate every novel compound, name, or inflection that the discrete-infinity property generates; subword tokenizers (Chapter 2) cope by composing arbitrary strings from a closed inventory of subword pieces. (b) Most n-grams of length 5+ are Zipfian-rare and never appear, even in trillion-token corpora; this is why count-based language models without smoothing collapse and why neural models that share parameters across contexts win. (c) Discrete infinity guarantees that any LLM generates fluent strings far outside its training distribution, including factually empty ones. Fluency comes from local statistical regularities; truth requires grounding the generation against an external state. The structural property of language is exactly what makes hallucination intrinsic rather than a bug to patch out.
