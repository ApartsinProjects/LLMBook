Exercise 2.2.1: BPE Merges by Hand Calculation

You train BPE on a tiny corpus where the words and their counts are: "low" x 5, "lower" x 2, "newest" x 6, "widest" x 3. Initialize each word as a sequence of characters with a word-end marker. (a) What is the most frequent pair after the first merge step, and what is its count? (b) After the first merge, what is the most frequent pair on the second step? (c) Continue for 3-4 merges and show the resulting tokens for "lowest". What does this exercise reveal about why BPE generalizes to unseen words?

Answer Sketch

(a) Pair "es" appears in "newest" (6) and "widest" (3) for a total count of 9, the highest. Merge "es" -> "es". (b) Now pair "est" (the merged "es" + "t") appears in "newest" (6) and "widest" (3) for 9 again, dominating. Merge "est". (c) Continuing might merge "lo" + "w" = "low" (count 7 from "low" + "lower"), then "low" + "er" = "lower" (count 2 alone, so unlikely to win early). For "lowest", which never appeared in training, BPE encodes greedily: "low" + "est", reusing two merges learned from other words. This is exactly why BPE handles unseen words: every learned merge is reusable across the entire vocabulary, so morphologically composed neologisms are tokenized into known pieces. The original Sennrich et al. 2016 paper showed this gives ~1.5 BLEU on rare-word translation.

Exercise 2.2.2: WordPiece vs. BPE Scoring Conceptual

Section 2.2 contrasts BPE (merge most frequent pair) with WordPiece (merge pair with highest $\frac{P(xy)}{P(x)P(y)}$). (a) Construct a synthetic counter-example where the two algorithms would pick different merges in the same step. (b) Why does WordPiece's scoring function correspond to maximizing the corpus likelihood under a unigram model? (c) Which algorithm tends to produce more "linguistically plausible" subword units, and what is the empirical evidence in practice?

Answer Sketch

(a) Suppose pair (e, s) has count 100 and unigram counts P(e)=10000, P(s)=10000; pair (q, u) has count 30 and P(q)=30, P(u)=30000. BPE picks (e, s). WordPiece's pointwise mutual information score: 100/(10000*10000) is tiny; 30/(30*30000) is much larger. WordPiece picks (q, u) because "q" almost always co-occurs with "u". (b) Maximizing $\log\frac{P(xy)}{P(x)P(y)}$ summed over the corpus is equivalent to the gain in unigram language model log-likelihood from treating xy as a single token. So WordPiece is a greedy likelihood maximizer over a unigram tokenizer family. (c) WordPiece tends to prefer rare, distinctive pairs (like "ing", "tion", and BERT's famous "##ization") that capture morphology. Empirically, on English the two algorithms produce similar downstream quality at matched vocabulary size; the differences show up more on morphologically rich languages and in the "Common Misconception" callout's reminder that data quality dominates algorithm choice.

Exercise 2.2.3: Unigram Viterbi Trace Calculation

The "Numerical Example: Viterbi Segmentation" callout walks through the Unigram model's segmentation algorithm. Given a vocabulary with these log-probabilities: "un" = -2, "happy" = -3, "happi" = -4, "ness" = -3, "happiness" = -8, "unhappiness" = -12, segment the input "unhappiness" by Viterbi. (a) What is the optimal segmentation and its score? (b) How does this differ from BPE's greedy segmentation, and why does Unigram often produce shorter token sequences? (c) What does the regularization parameter in SentencePiece's "unigram" mode do at inference time?

Answer Sketch

(a) Candidates: ["unhappiness"] = -12; ["un", "happiness"] = -2 + -8 = -10; ["un", "happi", "ness"] = -2 + -4 + -3 = -9; ["un", "happy", "ness"] would require "happy" to fit but the input is "happiness", so the relevant alternatives are the three above. The highest log-prob (least negative) is ["un", "happi", "ness"] at -9. (b) BPE applies merges greedily in learned order; if "happiness" is a learned merge, BPE will pick it even when a shorter higher-likelihood path exists with smaller pieces. Unigram does global Viterbi, choosing the segmentation that maximizes total probability. (c) SentencePiece's "subword regularization" samples from the top-k Viterbi paths during training instead of always taking the best one, which acts as input-side data augmentation; at inference you take the deterministic best segmentation. This regularization gives ~1 BLEU on translation tasks (Kudo 2018).

Exercise 2.2.4: Byte-Level BPE Robustness Failure Mode

GPT-2 introduced byte-level BPE: starting alphabet is 256 bytes instead of Unicode code points. (a) What category of inputs would crash a Unicode-level BPE tokenizer that byte-level handles gracefully? (b) Why does byte-level BPE produce slightly longer sequences for Chinese, Japanese, and Korean text than a Unicode-level alternative? (c) Tokenizer-free models (ByT5) skip BPE entirely and operate on raw bytes. Name the single biggest cost they pay and the use case where it is worth it.

Answer Sketch

(a) Inputs with rare or invalid Unicode: emoji combinations the tokenizer never saw, malformed UTF-8 from a buggy upstream, mixed-script text with combining characters. Unicode-level tokenizers either crash or emit UNK; byte-level always succeeds because every byte is in the starting alphabet. This is why GPT-2/3/4 and Llama use byte-level BPE in production. (b) A single CJK character is typically 3 UTF-8 bytes, and BPE may not merge cross-character byte sequences as efficiently as it merges English character pairs. So the same Chinese sentence costs more tokens with byte-level than with a Chinese-aware Unicode tokenizer; the "Multilingual Token Tax" callout in Section 2.1 documents this. (c) Tokenizer-free models pay 4-8x in sequence length, which means 16-64x in attention cost. Worth it for noisy multilingual web data, OCR output, or genomics where the "tokens" are bytes anyway and the tokenization choice would itself be a hyperparameter.
