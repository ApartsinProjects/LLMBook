Problem-Discovery Heuristics

Section 67.2

"The best LLM product idea is the one your customer cannot stop describing. Your job is to listen long enough to write it down."

Prompt, Discovery-Interview-Native AI Agent
Big Picture

Section 67.1 told you what an LLM-worthy problem looks like in the abstract. This section is about how to actually find one inside a real organization, where the obvious problems are usually taken and the valuable ones are buried in workflows nobody describes in their job title. The five heuristics below have repeatedly turned up real, fundable problems across enterprises in 2024-2026. They work because they exploit a recurring pattern: humans complain in language, and language is exactly what an LLM consumes for breakfast.

Prerequisites

This section assumes the ideation fundamentals from Section 67.1 and a working mental model of the LLM-agent capability landscape from Section 26.1.

67.2.1 The "I Wish I Had an Intern" Filter

Fun Fact

Harvey AI was founded by Winston Weinberg (a Latham & Watkins associate) and Gabriel Pereyra (ex-OpenAI/DeepMind), but the actual product hypothesis was workshopped at a YC dinner where Weinberg described "the most annoying part of being a first-year associate". The pitch deck that closed Harvey's Series A was reportedly 11 slides, and the line that made the deal was "BigLaw partners bill at $1500 an hour and use Word 2007".

Walk through any office and listen for the phrase "I just wish I had someone who could..." followed by something tedious, language-shaped, and not safety-critical. The speaker has already done the willingness-to-pay calculation: an intern would cost real money, and they would still be useful. That gap, between "what an intern would solve" and "what no one has automated," is the exact niche where LLMs in 2026 dominate.

The classic version of this complaint sounds like: "I just wish I had someone who could read these 200 emails and pull out the ones that mention contract renewal." Each piece of that sentence is a green flag. The input is unstructured text. The output is a filtered list. The task is boring enough that a smart 22-year-old would not be wasted on it. And the speaker has implicitly priced an intern at, say, $50k a year, which means a $200/month SaaS that does the same job has obvious gross margin.

The phrase to listen for varies by industry. In financial services it is "I wish someone could read these 10-Ks and flag the litigation footnotes." In healthcare back-offices it is "I wish someone could turn these faxed referrals into intake forms." In legal it is "I wish someone could redline this NDA against our template." Each version is a productized AI feature waiting to be shipped, and the user has already done your customer research for you.

Practical example: Harvey's "intern for lawyers"

Harvey AI, which closed a Series E in 2025 at a reported $5 billion valuation, was founded on exactly this insight. Co-founder Winston Weinberg was a Wall Street associate; his cofounder Gabriel Pereyra was a researcher at DeepMind. The first product was, in essence, "the AI intern I wished I had as a first-year associate": redlines, due-diligence extracts, deposition summaries. The product spent its first 18 months selling to law firms by re-running this single pitch in different verticals. The "I wish I had an intern" filter does not just find one product; it finds an entire wedge.

67.2.2 The Manual-Handoff Spotter

Every business process has moments where a human reads something written by another human and decides what to do next: route the support ticket, classify the lead, escalate the complaint, code the medical visit. These handoffs share four properties that make them irresistible LLM targets. They are bottlenecks (volume backs up at the human). They are variable (different humans decide differently). They are expensive (humans charge by the hour). And they are language-shaped (both input and output are written in human language).

The diagnostic exercise: ask the operations lead of any function for a flowchart of their team's workflow. Find every arrow that crosses from "machine produces text" to "human reads text and decides." Each arrow is a candidate. The high-value ones cross from external (customer-generated) to internal (action-bearing); the low-value ones are internal-to-internal and were probably automated long ago.

Intercom's 2025 Fin AI report measured this directly: in the customer-support function, the typical SaaS company has 6 to 9 distinct manual handoffs per ticket. Their Fin AI agent automates 4 to 5 of them, which is enough to drop the average ticket cost by roughly half. The point is not that AI replaces the team; it is that AI eats the language-shaped handoffs, which were always the bottleneck.

67.2.3 The Abandonment Trail

Wherever users quit mid-flow, there is a problem that could not be completed unaided. Often the answer is "the task got too complicated to figure out without help." A coding tutorial site loses 40 percent of users at the "set up your Python environment" step. A tax-prep flow loses 30 percent at "describe your home office for the deduction." A B2B SaaS onboarding loses half its users at "connect your data warehouse."

Each abandonment is a moment where a human, in real time, gave up. They needed a coach, a translator, or a hand-holder, and your product did not have one. That role, the just-in-time-assistant role, is exactly what an LLM is good at: take what the user has, take where they are stuck, take some context about what success looks like, and generate the next step in plain English.

The pattern is reliable enough to have a name. Paul Graham's "do things that don't scale" tradition tells you to solve one abandoning user manually and watch what happens. If you save them and they thank you, you have a real problem. Now build the AI assistant that does what you just did, but for all the others.

67.2.4 The Schema-Bridge Pattern

Whenever a process translates between two formats, vocabularies, or schemas, a language model can be the translator. "Take this customer email and turn it into a Jira ticket with the right labels." "Take this PDF medical record and populate the patient-intake form." "Take this Slack discussion and write the engineering RFC." Schema mismatch is the workhorse of B2B integrations, and LLMs in 2026 are the cheapest bridge ever invented.

This pattern dominates the unsexy middle of B2B SaaS: tools like Merge, Airbyte, and Workato in their 2025 product lines all added LLM-powered transformation layers because the alternative (writing 500 custom mappers per integration) does not scale and never did. The pattern's value is hidden because nobody markets "schema translation" as a feature; they market the resulting integration. But under the hood, the LLM is what made the unit economics work.

Key Insight: The schema bridge has the best risk profile of the five heuristics

Schema-bridge problems are unusually forgiving. The input and output formats are defined, so you have a deterministic correctness check. The cost of a wrong mapping is usually a downstream validation error, not a user-visible failure. And the volume tends to be high enough that even a 1-cent-per-call cost ceiling produces good gross margin. Teams new to LLM products should consider a schema-bridge use case as their first ship, before attempting open-ended generation or agentic workflows.

67.2.5 The Support-Ticket Audit

If the same five questions appear in your customer-support inbox every week and a competent human could answer each one in under three minutes, you have a textbook RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation, the pattern where an LLM answers using passages pulled from your own documentation rather than from its training data; covered in detail in Chapter 32) opportunity. The work is to pull the ticket logs, cluster them by topic, and pick the top three clusters where the answer lives in documentation you control. That is a launchable AI feature whose business case (the deflection rate times the cost per ticket) is calculable on a napkin.

Intercom's 2025 report and parallel data from Zendesk's State of AI report both find that 30 to 50 percent of tickets at mature SaaS companies fall into this pattern. That is not a marketing claim; it is a measured deflection rate against real ticket volumes. The implication: for any SaaS company with more than ~$10M ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue, the SaaS industry's headline revenue metric: the run-rate value of all active subscriptions), the ticket-audit heuristic produces a fundable AI feature in an afternoon.

Warning: do not confuse "common ticket" with "easy ticket"

The trap in the ticket-audit pattern is treating frequency as a proxy for tractability. Many high-frequency tickets are high-frequency precisely because they are hard. "My account is locked" appears in every SaaS inbox because the resolution often requires looking up account state, verifying identity, and triggering an internal workflow. An LLM that drafts a reply but cannot act is a worse experience than a human, not a better one. The win is in the high-frequency tickets where the answer is genuinely in your docs. Filter on both axes.

Combining the Heuristics

The five heuristics are not mutually exclusive. The best opportunities usually score on at least two of them. A customer-support assistant satisfies "I wish I had an intern" (every support manager has said this), "manual handoff" (humans reading tickets), and "support-ticket audit" (the data is right there) simultaneously. When you find a candidate problem that satisfies three of the five, you have something worth a 30-day MVP. When you find one that satisfies four, you have something worth a quarter of engineering attention.

The next section translates a candidate problem into a go/no-go decision: the bet-my-money test, and the explicit mapping from problem shape to the LLM capability that solves it. That mapping is what separates a six-week MVP from a six-month engineering swamp.

Key takeaway
  • "I wish I had an intern" surfaces problems where willingness-to-pay is already proven.
  • Manual handoffs in workflows are the highest-leverage targets in any organization.
  • Abandonment trails reveal moments where users would have paid for help.
  • Schema bridges have the best risk profile and the cleanest unit economics.
  • Support-ticket audits convert existing data into a calculable business case.
  • Score a candidate against at least three heuristics before committing engineering time.
