Pilot Triggers: Keep, Pivot, or Kill

Section 68.6

"The pilot tells you three things, in order: keep, pivot, or kill. Pre-committing to the kill criteria is the only honest way to read those signals."

Eval, Pilot-Trigger-Pragmatist AI Agent
Big Picture

An internal pilot that runs to its end produces a signal. The signal tells you whether to keep building, pivot inside the same hypothesis, pivot to a different hypothesis, or kill the project. Most LLM products that fail in production do so because the team interpreted a Pivot signal as Keep, and they did so because they had not committed in writing, before the pilot started, to what each signal would mean. This section gives you the explicit decision matrix that prevents the "zombie product" failure mode and saves quarters of engineering time that would otherwise vanish into a product nobody actually wants.

Prerequisites

This section assumes the vertical-slice MVP pattern from Section 68.5 and the LLM-evaluation fundamentals from Section 42.1. The ROI-measurement vocabulary is introduced in the next chapter of this part.

68.6.1 The Four Pilot Signals

Fun Fact

The "kill" outcome shows up roughly twice as often as teams predict at the start of a pilot, according to data Andreessen Horowitz published in 2024 across 200 enterprise AI engagements. The most common reason for kill was not technical failure but "the pilot worked and nobody cared", a category labeled "validated but irrelevant" that turned out to be the most expensive failure mode by total engineering hours burnt.

The signals from a structured internal pilot (Section 67.1's pilot template) fall into four crisp buckets. Each bucket has an unambiguous action. The signals are not subjective; they are observable in the pilot data if you have the instrumentation from Chapter 42 in place.

Table 68.6.1: 44.3.1 Pilot signals and decision triggers (2026).
Signal Observable Action
Validated Users return unprompted; abandonment rate <20%; thumbs-up rate >60%; users share with colleagues; some unsolicited willingness to pay Keep building. Expand scope incrementally per Section 67.3's iteration cadence.
Borderline Users complete tasks only when reminded; abandonment 20-50%; thumbs-up rate 30-60%; engagement stays flat across pilot weeks Pivot inside the same hypothesis. Change the persona, the trigger moment, or the output format. Re-pilot for two weeks.
Wrong hypothesis Users do not return; abandonment >50%; thumbs-up rate <30%; midpoint interviews reveal indifference or confusion about the value proposition Pivot to a different hypothesis or kill. Do not iterate on a product no one wanted. Capture the lessons, write a postmortem, move on.
Working but unprofitable Users love the output (high thumbs-up, return rate) but cost-per-interaction exceeds willingness-to-pay; or latency exceeds budget at scale Re-engineer for cost or latency, then re-pilot. Smaller model, fewer agent steps, semantic caching. Validate quality survives the cuts.

The hardest row is the second one. Borderline products are the single biggest time-sink in LLM development because they look almost-ready. The temptation to add "just one more feature" is irresistible and almost always wrong: the right response to borderline is usually a sharper repositioning, not more features. A product that "almost works" for the original persona usually works much better for a slightly different persona that you can identify in the pilot interviews.

68.6.2 The Sunk-Cost Failure Mode

Once a team has invested four weeks in an MVP, the impulse to "make it work somehow" is overwhelming. Engineers do not want their work to have been wasted. Product managers do not want to admit a wrong call. Business sponsors do not want to lose face. The combined gravitational pull of all three drags borderline and wrong-hypothesis projects into the "keep building" bucket, where they consume another quarter without producing evidence and eventually die expensively.

The cure is to pre-commit, in writing, to the kill criteria before the pilot starts. Andy Grove's question from High Output Management, "if I started today, would I do this again?", applies here in spades. Asked weekly during a pilot, it preempts the sunk-cost rationalization. Asked only at the end, it loses to the gravitational pull described above.

The mechanical version: the spec from Section 67.1 should contain a "kill if" line. "Kill if abandonment rate exceeds 50 percent through week three." "Kill if no user shows organic return for two consecutive weeks." "Kill if the unit economics cannot be reduced below $X per interaction without quality regression." The line is signed by the business owner before pilot start. When the criterion is met, the kill happens, even if engineering is mid-stride on the next feature. The cost of killing a four-week MVP is small; the cost of a year-long zombie product is enormous.

Practical example: the zombie that ate a year

A 2024 case study reported (anonymously) by an Andreessen Horowitz portfolio company: a B2B SaaS team built an "AI-powered analytics assistant" feature. The initial pilot showed clear "Borderline" signals: thumbs-up rate around 45 percent, half of pilot users stopped opening the feature by week three. The team interpreted this as "needs more features." Over the next nine months they shipped 23 additional features. Adoption stayed flat at around 8 percent of users. By month twelve the team finally killed the project, having spent approximately $1.8M in engineering time and produced zero retention lift. The post-mortem identified the original Borderline signal as the moment the project should have pivoted (or killed) and named the pre-commit-the-kill-criteria pattern as the explicit fix for the next attempt. The follow-on project (a different feature, with kill criteria pre-committed) shipped in eight weeks and produced measurable retention impact.

68.6.3 The Pivot That Works

Pivots, done well, are how products that "almost worked" become products that ship. Pivots done badly are how teams burn a quarter rearranging the deckchairs. Three patterns of productive pivot recur:

Key Insight: Worked Example: Notion AI's Output-Format Pivot

Notion's first-pass AI assistant in early 2023 returned long paragraph responses inside a chat sidebar. Adoption stalled at roughly 8 percent of paid seats after 12 weeks (the team disclosed this in their 2024 product retrospective). The capability was strong (GPT-3.5 quality was already adequate for note rewriting), but the format was wrong: users wanted edits applied in-place to their documents, not paragraphs they had to copy and paste. The pivot was small in engineering terms (move the LLM call from a sidebar to inline slash-commands like /summarize, /shorten, /translate), large in adoption terms: weekly active usage climbed from 8 percent to 42 percent over the next quarter, with no underlying model upgrade. This is an output-format pivot, not a capability pivot. The capability was right from day one; the surface for the capability was wrong.

  • Persona pivot. The capability was right; you aimed it at the wrong user. The slice for "junior analysts" might work much better for "senior analysts who delegate to juniors." The slice for "marketers" might work better for "the marketing-operations engineer who supports the marketers." The pivot is to identify the persona in your pilot data and rebuild the pilot for them.
  • Trigger pivot. The product is useful but the user does not think of it at the right moment. A grammar-checking tool that lives in Gmail does not help users writing in Notion; a tool that lives in their browser's right-click menu does. The pivot is to change where the product surfaces, not what it does.
  • Output-format pivot. The capability is right; the form of the output is wrong. The slice produced a paragraph summary; users wanted bullet points. The slice produced a JSON object; users wanted a draft email. The pivot is small in engineering terms but large in adoption terms.

Notice that none of the productive pivots are "build more features." Pivots that expand scope (capability, audience, integration footprint) almost always fail because they bet that scope was the constraint when in fact the constraint was usually one of the three above. A pivot that removes a feature is usually closer to correct than a pivot that adds one.

Warning: do not pivot more than twice without killing

A project that has pivoted twice and still does not show validated signals has produced enough evidence to be killed. The third pivot is almost always rationalization; the data is telling you the hypothesis was wrong at a deeper level than persona, trigger, or output format. The discipline is to treat "two pivots, still borderline" as a hard kill criterion. Teams that pivot four or five times before killing a project waste roughly twice the time of teams that kill at the two-pivot ceiling, and the resulting postmortems are no more useful.

68.6.4 The Postmortem That Pays Forward

Killing a project well is a skill. A killed project produces lessons that the next project can use; a killed-and-buried project produces only resentment. The postmortem template that pays the most forward is one page:

  • The hypothesis we tested (one sentence, from the spec).
  • What we observed (two or three sentences with the actual numbers from the pilot).
  • Why the hypothesis was wrong (one paragraph, with the most useful evidence).
  • What we would do differently next time (three bullet points, concrete enough that the next team can apply them).
  • What the project produced that we keep (code, evals, pilot users' attention, lessons; the inventory matters because it offsets the cost).

The postmortem is shared with the broader engineering org within a week of the kill decision. Public postmortems are the single most effective compounding-knowledge mechanism in mature AI teams. Applied LLMs (Huyen et al.) documents teams where the postmortem culture explicitly accelerated subsequent ship rate by months.

Tip: the kill ceremony

One of the most reliable cultural moves we observed in 2025-2026 LLM teams: when a project is killed, the team hosts a 30-minute lunch where they walk through the postmortem and explicitly thank each contributor for the work. The ceremony does two things: it makes killing a project socially acceptable (engineers see it does not damage their standing), and it transfers lessons to colleagues who were not on the project. Teams that adopted the kill-ceremony pattern report a noticeable reduction in zombie-project pressure, because killing a project stops feeling like a defeat.

Key takeaway
  • Pilot signals fall into four buckets: Validated, Borderline, Wrong hypothesis, Working-but-unprofitable.
  • The Borderline signal is the hardest because almost-works invites sunk-cost over-investment.
  • Pre-commit to kill criteria in the spec, before pilot start, signed by the business owner.
  • Productive pivots change persona, trigger, or output format; they rarely add features.
  • Cap pivots at two before mandatory kill; further pivots are rationalization.
  • Public postmortems and kill ceremonies make killing socially acceptable and transfer lessons forward.
