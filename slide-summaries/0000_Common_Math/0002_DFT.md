## Slide 1 — FOURIER TRANSFORM
Title slide opening the Fourier-transform module. The deck is framed as intuition-only with no rigorous derivations, and the authors warn readers to expect abuse of notation, simplifications, and small inaccuracies. The tone is set to favor conceptual continuity over formal proof, which prepares readers for the heavy use of analogies (vectors, basis changes, eigenfunctions) that will follow.

## Slide 2 — MOTIVATION FOR FREQUENCY DOMAIN ANALYSIS
Section divider introducing why frequency-domain thinking is needed.

## Slide 3 — Vector Spaces
Establishes the vector-space vocabulary that the rest of the deck will recycle. Polar and Cartesian 2D vectors are compared as two coordinate systems describing the same geometric object, and coordinates are reframed as coefficients of a chosen basis. The slide then generalizes to n-dimensional vectors, which is the bridge needed before extending the same idea to function spaces.

## Slide 4 — Changing Basis
Motivates the central operational idea of the deck: changing basis can reveal structure or enable compression. Two vectors are rotated into a basis aligned with their common direction; the second coordinate becomes tiny and can be dropped with little distortion, which saves storage. Conversely, dropping the first coefficient isolates each vector's deviation from the common direction, a hint at later signal-processing decompositions.

## Slide 5 — Linear operator
Defines linear operators as maps that respect a space's sum and scalar-multiplication operators, which may be defined differently in different spaces. Linearity is positioned as broadly expressive enough to model or approximate many real-world phenomena. The key payoff stated: a linear operator is fully determined by its action on a basis, which dramatically reduces what must be measured or specified.

## Slide 6 — Eigenvectors(eigenfunctions) of linear operator
Pushes the basis idea further by introducing eigenvectors and eigenvalues. Switching to the eigenbasis turns a linear operator into a diagonal action defined by eigenvalues alone. The caveat is noted that different operators (for example different matrices) generally have different eigenvectors, which makes side-by-side comparison awkward; that obstacle will later be resolved for LTI operators, which all share the same eigenbasis.

## Slide 7 — Infinite Dimension Spaces
Lifts the vector-space framework to function spaces. "Good-behaving" functions form an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space; the Taylor series is offered as the first concrete example, with polynomials as a countable basis and Taylor coefficients as the coordinates. The slide stresses that this representation is local, valid near a point t0, which sets up the next slide's search for a global basis.

## Slide 8 — Infinite Dimensional Spaces
Asks whether a basis exists that represents a good function everywhere, not just near a point. The answer is yes, but it requires an uncountable basis of shifted Dirac deltas indexed by a continuous parameter n. Infinite sums become integrals, and the appealing consequence is that any linear operator needs only to be characterized on delta functions, an easier object than the full polynomial machinery.

## Slide 9 — Dirac Delta Function
Introduces the Dirac delta as a mathematical object impossible to visualize directly but provable through limits. The slide draws an analogy to a function continuous everywhere yet differentiable nowhere, to ease the reader's discomfort with non-classical objects. The accompanying figure shows the standard Gaussian-limit definition: δ0(t) = lim(σ→0) (1/(σ√π)) e^(−x²/σ²), depicted as a low-amplitude Gaussian bump with parameter a=1/1 that is meant to be squeezed toward an infinite spike as σ shrinks.

## Slide 10 — Properties of Dirac Delta Function
Lists the three operational properties needed for the rest of the deck: the delta is zero almost everywhere, integrates to one, and obeys the sifting property ∫ f(t)δ(t−τ)dt = f(τ). The embedded figure reinforces the spike-with-unit-area picture. These three properties are the only tools required to manipulate deltas in subsequent derivations.

## Slide 11 — Shifted Dirac Delta Function
Treats the family of shifted deltas {δ(t−n)} as a basis with continuous index n, contrasting with the natural-number index of polynomials. Any good function is reconstructed as an integral over this family, and the coefficient at index n is simply f(n) by the sifting property. This sets up the analogy that the spatial/time-domain representation is itself an expansion in a delta basis.

## Slide 12 — Linear-Time Invariant(LTI) Operator
Introduces LTI operators as the joint linear and time-invariant class. Time-invariance plus linearity force the operator to act through convolution with a single function: the impulse response h(t), defined as the operator's response to δ(t). Analyzing or specifying an LTI operator therefore reduces to knowing its impulse response, a major simplification.

## Slide 13 — Discrete case
Repeats the construction for infinite-length discrete signals. The basis is the Kronecker delta family, the signal expansion becomes a sum, and a linear operator on the sequence reduces to discrete convolution with the impulse response. The parallel structure between continuous and discrete formulations is highlighted, which the deck will later exploit for the DFT.

## Slide 14 — Recall: Filtering
A reminder slide showing the spatial-domain filtering formulation. The diagram depicts an image f(x,y) with the origin at the top-left, x increasing downward and y rightward, and a small 3×3 neighborhood window highlighted at a generic pixel (x,y). The bottom equations write T[δ0(k)] = H(k) implying T[f(k)] = Σ f(n)h(k−n), and the 2D analog g(x,y) = Σ Σ w(s,t) f(x+s, y+t). The point is that filtering is convolution; the equations and the moving-window picture are the same idea in two notations.

## Slide 15 — Example
A concrete worked example of LTI on a discrete signal. Averaging a window is presented as a simple LTI operator whose impulse response is a rectangular indicator (uniform weights summing to one), and the output is computed by discrete convolution with that response. This grounds the abstract impulse-response language in a familiar operation.

## Slide 16 — LTI operator on functions
Pivots the deck toward the Fourier transform by asking for the eigenfunctions of an LTI operator. The remarkable result is announced in plain language: all LTI operators share the same eigenfunctions, and there are uncountably many of them, but the trick requires moving to the complex domain. Complex eigenvalues will carry physically meaningful information, and once basis vectors are universal, LTI operators become trivial to compare, define, and combine — each one is fully described by its eigenvalues.

## Slide 17 — Complex numbers
A compact reminder of complex-number machinery needed for the next slides. The slide stresses that complex numbers have no direct physical embodiment but provide a notational trick that simplifies operations on real quantities. It lists the imaginary unit j, a generic complex number, the Euler identity e^(jθ) = cos θ + j sin θ, and complex conjugation.

## Slide 18 — Eigenfunctions of LTI
Proposes complex exponentials e^(j2πut) as candidate eigenfunctions of LTI operators. The index u is continuous and is identified with frequency, and the corresponding period is 1/u. This is the form whose action under any LTI operator will turn out to be scalar multiplication.

## Slide 19 — Eigenfunctions of LTI operator
Verifies the eigen-relation. Applying an LTI operator with impulse response h(t) to e^(j2πut) and substituting a change of variables yields the same exponential multiplied by ∫ h(τ)e^(−j2πuτ)dτ, a complex eigenvalue that depends only on u. This integral is the Fourier transform of h, which closes the loop between LTI theory and Fourier analysis.

## Slide 20 — Back to real domain
Translates the complex result into the real signals that practitioners actually measure. Applying an LTI operator to cos(2πut) and invoking trig identities shows that the output is the same cosine scaled in amplitude and shifted in phase. The cosine is therefore almost an eigenfunction: each LTI operator is fully specified by how much it scales and phase-shifts each frequency, and these two real numbers are conveniently packaged in one complex coefficient.

## Slide 21 — Frequency Response of LTI
Defines the frequency response of an LTI operator. If a signal is expanded in the complex-exponential basis with coefficients c(u), then the output is the same expansion with coefficients H(u)c(u), where H(u) is the frequency response. Operating in the frequency domain is now reduced to pointwise multiplication, which is the central practical payoff of the deck.

## Slide 22 — Frequency Domain Representation
Wraps up the LTI thread before the deck shifts to DFT. The eigenfunctions of all LTI operators form a basis; using that basis, an LTI operator is just multiplication of each coefficient by a complex eigenvalue, which in the real world corresponds to scaling and phase-shifting each cosine of a given frequency.

## Slide 23 — FREQUENCY DOMAIN
Section divider marking the shift from theory to the explicit Fourier representations.

## Slide 24 — Continuous/Discrete Representation
Lays out the menu of Fourier representations matched to signal types. The Fourier integral represents any good function on the line; the Fourier series represents any good periodic function; the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) represents any periodic discrete sequence of period N. The slide closes with the standard finite-length convention: a finite discrete sequence is treated as a single period of a periodic discrete function.

## Slide 25 — Different Forms of Fourier Series
Compares the equivalent ways to write a Fourier series. The complex-exponential form uses a single complex coefficient per harmonic and is the most compact. Two real-coefficient forms exist: separate coefficients for cosines and sines, and the polar form using amplitude and phase per harmonic. All three carry the same information, repackaged.

## Slide 26 — Representations are equivalent
Makes the bookkeeping between the three forms explicit. Two-amplitude (cos/sin) representation is shown to be equivalent to single-amplitude-and-phase representation via Pythagoras and arctan, and either can be obtained from the magnitude and argument of the complex coefficient. The slide is essentially a conversion table.

## Slide 27 — A sum of cosines
Visualizes how cosines stack to build an arbitrary periodic signal. The slide shows two columns of small plots. The left column shows the target rectangular pulse and successive harmonic components f1, f2, f3 in isolation. The right column shows running partial sums f0, f0+f1, f0+...+f2, f0+...+f3, and f0+...+f6, each progressively closer to the target square wave. A pink callout box summarizes: f(target) = f1 + f2 + f3 + ... + fn + .... The building block is written as d_n cos(n t + θ_n).

## Slide 28 — Frequency Domain
A worked example linking time waveforms to frequency spectra. The equation s(t) = cos(2π f t) + (1/3) cos(2π · 3 f t) is decomposed visually: the composite waveform on the left equals a slow cosine plus a faster cosine of one-third amplitude on the right (linked by green "=" and "+" signs). Below the time plots, a small spectrum plot shows two impulses on the frequency axis at f and 3f with heights 1 and 0.3, illustrating that frequency-domain representation is just a list of amplitudes per frequency bin.

## Slide 29 — DFT
Section divider opening the DFT formalism proper.

## Slide 30 — 1D Fourier Transform
Defines the continuous 1D Fourier transform and its inverse. F(u) deconstructs f(x) into complex coefficients and the inverse synthesizes f(x) from F(u); F(u) and f(x) form a Fourier pair. The transform is linear, and F(0) equals the signal average (the DC component), an intuitively useful anchor.

## Slide 31 — 1D Discrete Fourier Transform
States the 1D DFT and its inverse for length-M sequences. The 1/M normalization may be split between forward and inverse transforms in different conventions, as long as the product of the two multipliers equals 1/M. A key advantage over the continuous case is asserted: the DFT and its inverse always exist for finite discrete functions, with no convergence caveats.

## Slide 32 — Negative Frequencies in DFT
Clarifies the negative-frequency convention that confuses many newcomers to the DFT. In the Fourier series and integral, negative frequencies are explicit; in the DFT they wrap around so that index M−k carries the same information as index −k. The DC coefficient is F(0), and for real signals F(k) and F(M−k) form a complex-conjugate pair, which is the source of the Hermitian symmetry of the spectrum.

## Slide 33 — Frequency Domain Coefficients
Re-emphasizes that each DFT coefficient is built from every input sample weighted by sines and cosines at one frequency. The index u runs over the frequency domain and each F(u) is a frequency component, while the original samples f(t) live in the time domain as time components. The terminology of "components" in two dual domains is being installed for use in the rest of the deck.

## Slide 34 — Basis Functions
Describes the DFT basis functions concretely. Each basis function fits an integer number of periods into M samples. Index u=1 has a single period spanning the window, u=M−1 also has a single period (the negative-frequency counterpart), and u=M/2 has the highest representable rate of alternation, two samples per period. This is the conceptual setup for the Nyquist discussion that follows.

## Slide 35 — (untitled cosine-waves grid)
A titleless visual showing a 3×3 grid of cosine waveforms labeled "Cosine Waves", each plotted on a 16-sample axis. Frequencies increase across rows and columns, from the slowest single-period cosine through medium frequencies and up to the highest-frequency Nyquist case at the bottom-right, where the sampled cosine alternates sign at every other sample. The grid visually inventories the DFT basis functions introduced on the previous slide.

## Slide 36 — Spatial Resolution of DFT
Defines the sampling structure underlying the DFT. M samples are drawn from a continuous function, not necessarily at integer values k=0,...,M−1. The slide names the sampling frequency fs and the sampling period Ts = 1/fs as the bridge between physical units and the abstract DFT indexing.

## Slide 37 — Frequency Resolution of DFT
Derives the DFT's frequency resolution from the spatial sampling. The DFT fits M periods of the basis functions into the sampling time T, so the available physical frequencies are k/T Hz with the highest being M/(2T). The frequency resolution Δf and the spatial resolution Ts are inversely proportional: shorter records mean coarser frequency bins, and finer frequency resolution requires either a longer record or more samples M.

## Slide 38 — Ambiguity problem
A visual setup for aliasing. The top plot (a) shows discrete samples at heights +0.866 and −0.866 alternating in a pattern that is consistent with two very different sinusoids. The bottom plot (b) overlays two candidate continuous sinusoids that both pass exactly through those samples: a slow black sine wave and a much faster gray sine wave. The slide visually argues that the same sample set is ambiguous between many frequencies, which the next slides formalize.

## Slide 39 — Ambiguity
Formalizes the ambiguity demonstrated visually. Given a sampling frequency fs, the digital sequence representing cos(2π f t) is identical to the digital sequence for infinitely many other sinusoids whose frequencies differ from f by integer multiples of fs (with k, m possibly negative, per the speaker note). This is the algebraic statement of aliasing.

## Slide 40 — Sampling and aliasing
A purely visual slide showing the same image-like 2D test pattern sampled at five resolutions: 256×256, 128×128, 64×64, 32×32, and 16×16. As resolution drops, the fine high-frequency stripes degrade into spurious low-frequency Moiré-like patterns, illustrating spatial aliasing in two dimensions. The progression makes the failure mode of under-sampling visceral.

## Slide 41 — Ambiguity in frequency domain
A diagram showing aliasing from the spectral side. The top panel shows a continuous bandlimited spectrum S(f) supported on [−W, W]. The middle panel shows the spectrum X(f) of the sampled signal when Ts > 1/(2W): copies c_{−2}, c_{−1}, c_0, c_1, c_2 appear at spacing 1/Ts and do not overlap. The bottom panel shows Ts < 1/(2W): the copies now overlap, with the overlap regions marked "Aliasing" in solid blue. A boxed formula at the bottom records f_max = M/(2T) = fs/2, the highest frequency representable without aliasing.

## Slide 42 — Nyquist sampling frequency
Derives the Nyquist condition from the replica picture. For a signal band [−fW, fW], avoiding overlap of spectral replicas requires fs > 2 fW, defining the Nyquist sampling frequency. Equivalently, for a fixed sampling rate fs, the maximum representable frequency without aliasing is fW < fs/2. The accompanying figure (parts a and b) shows the band of interest and the replicas at fs and 2fs, with the second example fixing fs = 6 kHz to make the spacing concrete.

## Slide 43 — Rectangular Pulse
Introduces the rectangular pulse and its Fourier integral. The pulse rect(t, τ) of width τ has Fourier transform proportional to the sinc function, sin(πfτ)/(πfτ) scaled by τ. This pair will be reused as the canonical example of an essentially time-limited signal with a wide spectrum.

## Slide 44 — Sinc function
A visual rendering of the Fourier pair from the previous slide. The left panel shows the rectangular pulse x(t) = rect(t, τ) of height 1 supported on [−τ/2, τ/2]. A double-arrow labels the Fourier-pair relationship, and the right panel shows X(jΩ) as a sinc with peak τ at the origin, zero crossings at ±2π/τ, ±4π/τ, ±6π/τ, and progressively damped side lobes. The picture makes the time-frequency duality visible.

## Slide 45 — Parseval's Theorem
States Parseval's theorem geometrically. A rotation preserves the length of a vector, and the move between the spatial and frequency domains is exactly such a rotation because both bases are orthonormal. Energy is therefore conserved between the two representations, which is the deck's geometric reading of Parseval.

## Slide 46 — LTI AND Fourier TRANSFORM
Section divider preparing the unification of the two threads (LTI on one side, Fourier on the other).

## Slide 47 — Back To LTI: The Convolution Theorem
Re-states the dual description of LTI operators: impulse response in the time domain, frequency response in the frequency domain. The slide poses the question that the convolution theorem will answer: how exactly are h(x) and H(u) related?

## Slide 48 — The Convolution Theorem
Sketches the proof. Taking the Fourier transform of the convolution f∗h, using the Fourier transform of a delta, and applying an LTI operator with impulse/frequency response (h, H) shows that the Fourier transform of the output is H(u) times the Fourier transform of the input. Convolution in time corresponds to multiplication in frequency, and H(u) and h(x) are a Fourier pair.

## Slide 49 — Summary
Recapitulates the entire deck. LTI operators model many useful phenomena; they are simply described in time as convolution with the impulse response, and even more simply in frequency. All LTI operators share the same eigenfunctions, so a single complex eigenvalue per frequency (the frequency response) suffices to specify any of them, with the physical meaning of scaling and phase-shifting cosines. Representing a signal in the LTI eigenbasis is exactly the Fourier transform, and the impulse and frequency responses of an LTI operator are a Fourier pair.

Each Fourier-image panel is mostly black with a small symmetric pair of bright dots whose position from the center indicates the spatial frequency and orientation of the stripes. Demonstrates linearity in 2D visually. Two basis-like intensity images, one with vertical stripes and one with diagonal stripes, sum to a more complex composite pattern. ## Slide 53 — 2D Fourier Transform
States the continuous 2D Fourier transform and its inverse over both spatial axes, with frequency variables (u, v). The construction is the natural product of two 1D transforms.

## Slide 54 — 2D DFT
States the discrete 2D DFT and its inverse for an M×N image, with the standard 1/(MN) normalization in the forward direction or the symmetric 1/√(MN) split. (u, v) index frequency and (x, y) index space. The small inset on the right shows the spatial appearance of a single 2D basis function (diagonal stripes), grounding the formula visually.

## Slide 55 — Spectrum, Phase, Power Spectrum
Defines the three quantities derived from a complex spectrum F(u, v): the spectrum |F|, the phase arg F, and the power spectrum |F|². These are the displayable real-valued summaries used throughout image processing.

## Slide 56 — Properties of 2D DFT
Lists the operational conventions for image DFTs. Multiplying f(x,y) by (−1)^(x+y) before the DFT centers the DC component in the displayed spectrum (assuming M, N even). The DC coefficient equals the image mean times MN, real inputs yield Hermitian symmetry F(−u, −v) = F*(u, v), and the spatial/frequency resolutions follow the 1D relations on each axis.

## Slide 57 — Frequency vs. Spatial Domain
Gives the qualitative reading of image spectra. Frequency directly corresponds to rate of change of gray levels, so the Fourier transform reflects patterns of intensity variation: positions near the origin (u=0, v=0) encode slow variations such as broad gradients, and positions far from the origin encode fast variations such as edges and fine texture. This is the practical lens through which image spectra are interpreted.

## Slide 58 — Example
A captioned image-processing example. On the left, a SEM (scanning electron microscope) image of a damaged integrated circuit is shown above its Fourier (power) spectrum, which features a bright central DC peak with a prominent star-like cross of energy along the dominant edge directions of the circuit traces. The caption credits Dr. J. M. Hudak at the Brockhouse Institute for Materials Research, McMaster University, and is labeled "Figure 4.4." The example illustrates how regular geometric structure in an image manifests as concentrated linear streaks in the 2D spectrum.

## Deck-level takeaway
The deck builds the Fourier transform from first principles by treating signals as vectors and operators as matrices on those vectors. The key conceptual chain is: vectors → basis changes → eigenbases → infinite-dimensional function spaces → Dirac-delta and Kronecker-delta bases → LTI operators as convolution with an impulse response → the discovery that all LTI operators share the same eigenfunctions (complex exponentials, that is, cosines with phase). Once that universal eigenbasis is in hand, every LTI operator is reduced to a complex frequency response H(u), and the Fourier transform is simply the change of basis into this representation. Convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency.

The second half operationalizes this view. The 1D DFT and its inverse are introduced together with the negative-frequency wrap-around, basis-function picture, and spatial-versus-frequency resolution trade-off. The Nyquist/aliasing story is told twice, once with a hand-drawn sample-ambiguity sketch and once with the canonical "spectral-replica overlap" diagram, leading to fs > 2 fW. The treatment then closes with 2D extensions used in image processing: 2D cosines as oriented gratings, additivity of spectra, the M×N DFT, the spectrum/phase/power-spectrum vocabulary, and an SEM example showing how image structure shows up as oriented streaks in the spectrum. The narrative thread is deliberately one of intuition and reuse: every later object (DFT, Nyquist, convolution theorem, 2D DFT) is justified by the same vector-space-and-eigenbasis argument introduced on slides 3-6.
