## Slide 1 — HMM and Bayesian Networks
Title slide for the lecture on Hidden Markov Models and Bayesian Networks. Sets up the two-pillar agenda: a class of probabilistic models for sequences (HMM) and a more general graphical-model framework (BN).

## Slide 2 — Probabilistic Sequences
Introduces the object of study: time-dependent random variables (time series), distinguishing regular vs. irregular sampling (sensor data measured every 1 sec versus once a year), and broadening to *ordered sequences* whose index is not time, such as words in a sentence or topics per sentence.

## Slide 3 — Models for Probabilistic Sequences
States the three things any probabilistic sequence model must provide: a *type of model* (HMM, BN, etc.), a *learning procedure* for the model's parameters (e.g., transition probabilities), and an *inference procedure* for estimating future events (e.g., the most probable next state).

## Slide 4 — Joint Probability
Frames the modeling challenge: for a sequence of four states the joint probability `P(W, X, Y, Z)` can in principle depend on every other variable. The slide motivates *graphical models* as a tool for expressing dependency assumptions concisely, naming HMM and Bayesian Networks (BN) as instances.

## Slide 5 — Hidden Markov Model
Defines an HMM: a sequence of observations (symbols from an alphabet or real-valued variables) indexed by `t`. The "hidden" part is that each observation is generated by a hidden state; the "Markov" part is that the next state depends only on the previous one. Embedded image-rendered formula sketches the joint distribution of states and observables.

## Slide 6 — Hidden Markov Model (parameters)
Enumerates HMM assumptions and parameters: hidden discrete state taking K values, K-by-K transition probability matrix, an initial-state distribution, and a K-by-L emission matrix when there are L possible observation values.

## Slide 7 — HMM: Modifications
Two natural extensions: for real-valued outputs, model emissions as Gaussian (or any continuous distribution); allow input variables, in which case HMM becomes a special case of a more general Bayesian network.

## Slide 8 — Factorization
Joint distributions on sequences can always be factored. The slide introduces conditional-independence factorizations of the form `p(W, Z | X, Y) = p(W | Y) p(Z | X, Y)` — that is, "knowing X and Y, knowing W tells you nothing further about Z." Embedded images show the corresponding factor graphs.

## Slide 9 — Proof
A formula-heavy slide (no extracted text), presumably containing the algebraic derivation of the factorization claim from the previous slide.

## Slide 10 — Bayesian Network
Defines a BN as a graphical representation of a joint distribution's factorization: each variable is a node, and a directed arc from A to B indicates that B is conditioned on A in the factorization. Two diagrams illustrate the convention.

## Slide 11 — Bayesian Network (d-separation)
States the core conditional-independence property: each node is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents. Generalizes via *d-separation*: disjoint sets A and B are conditionally independent given C if C d-separates them; this happens when, on every undirected path between A and B, there is a node D such that either (a) D has converging arrows and neither D nor its descendants are in C, or (b) D does not have converging arrows and is in C.

## Slide 12 — Bayesian Network (common-effect example)
Walks through the classic "explaining-away" case with three causes ("dead battery" A, "no gas" B, "car won't start" C) and structure A → C ← B. A and B are marginally independent but become dependent given C. The slide also enumerates the other three connection types (chain, reversed chain, common cause).

## Slide 13 — Bayesian Network (DAGs and I-maps)
Factorization implies strict ordering, so any BN is a DAG. Many BNs are consistent with the same distribution; a BN G is an *independency map* (I-map) for P if every d-separation in G is a conditional independence in P. A *minimal I-map* is one from which no arcs can be deleted without breaking the I-map property.

## Slide 14 — Belief Propagation (overview)
Introduces belief propagation as an efficient algorithm for computing marginal and conditional probabilities on acyclic underlying graphs. For graphs with cycles, the more general *junction tree* algorithm is used. Given evidence (a set of nodes with observed values) and known conditional probabilities `p(n | parents(n))`, the goal is to update the marginal `p(n | e)` for every variable.

## Slide 15 — Belief Propagation (message passing)
On acyclic graphs each node separates the network into two parts. The algorithm splits evidence into "above" and "below" a node and uses message passing: each K-valued discrete node sends two K-dimensional messages — one to its parents (conditional probability of its descendants' evidence given the node) and one to its children (conditional probability of the node given its ancestors' evidence).

## Slide 16 — Some math
Formula-image slide that fills in the BP message equations for the single-child and single-parent cases, with the explicit "message from child" and "message from parents" labels and the parameter set.

## Slide 17 — Dynamic Bayesian Networks
An HMM is a special case of a *dynamic Bayesian network*: a BN designed for time series in which directed arcs flow forward in time. The slide flags two extensions: higher-order interactions (using `n` past values), and *linear-Gaussian state-space models* (Kalman filters), which allow continuous state variables.

## Slide 18 — State-Space Model
Defines the continuous-state version: current state is a K-dimensional real-valued vector `X_t`; transition and emission probabilities decompose into deterministic and stochastic components. The linear-Gaussian special case is named (and presumably formula-illustrated in the embedded images).

## Slide 19 — Learning and Inference
Frames the *Bayesian* learning of the network: priors over models `P(M)` and over parameters per model (transition/emission for HMM), a dataset D of observations, and the posterior over models and parameters. The slide separates "learn parameters", "predict variables", "average unknowns".

## Slide 20 — Inference
Predicting the next observation given data: Bayesian prediction yields a *predictive distribution* (formula-rendered as images).

## Slide 21 — Maximum Likelihood Estimation
Switches from full Bayesian to point-estimate ML: assume the model structure is known, find parameters maximizing the likelihood. *Complete data* means all variables are observed.

## Slide 22 — ML with Complete Data
Data are IID time series. The slide gives the likelihood and log-likelihood (formula images), assumes a BN structure, and identifies `pa(j)` as the parents of node j — laying out the per-node factorization that makes ML decompose into local estimations.

## Slide 23 — ML with complete data (counts and normalize)
When data are factored, each factor is estimated from data. For discrete Y with K values, parameters are tables of conditional probabilities estimated by simple count-and-normalize.

## Slide 24 — ML with Hidden Variables: EM
With hidden X, the log-likelihood does not decompose nicely. *Observation*: any distribution Q(X) defines a lower bound on the log-likelihood (via Jensen's inequality applied to the concave log function). This sets up the Expectation-Maximization framework.

## Slide 25 — EM algorithm
EM alternates between maximizing the lower bound F with respect to Q (the *E-step*, which makes F equal to the log-likelihood by choosing Q = posterior over X) and with respect to the parameters (the *M-step*, which maximizes the first term of F).

## Slide 26 — Learning HMM using EM
Specializes EM to HMM. The slide spells out the log-likelihood, the one-hot encoding of the K-valued state (`state=2` → `[0,1,0,0,0,0]`), the transition probability matrix, the initial-state distribution, and the discrete-emission case.

## Slide 27 — Baum-Welch algorithm
The instance of EM that runs on HMM is called Baum-Welch. The slide names parameters and the per-step expectations (over the posterior of X) needed for the M-step, motivating the next slide.

## Slide 28 — Forward-Backward algorithm
The expectations Baum-Welch needs come from running belief propagation on the HMM, where parameters are fixed and some variables are observed. The slide introduces the *forward pass* recursion.

## Slide 29 — Forward-Backward algorithm (backward pass)
Completes the algorithm with the *backward pass* recursion. The product of forward and backward messages gives the posterior expectations needed by EM.

## Slide 30 — Viterbi Algorithm
For the related task of finding the *most probable* state sequence (not the posterior expectations), replace the summation in forward-backward with maximization — this is the Viterbi algorithm.

## Slide 31 — Learning state-space using EM
Specializes the same EM machinery to the continuous-state Gaussian state-space model: log-probability under a Gaussian with covariance R, the fully-observed case, and the hidden-variable case (which yields Kalman-filter style equations).

## Slide 32 — Limitations of HMM
HMM and linear-Gaussian state-space are special cases of DBNs. Linear-Gaussian assumes both linearity and Gaussianity. HMM has a fundamental scaling problem: if the true underlying state is M factors with K values each, then a single HMM needs `K^M` states and `K^(2M)` transition parameters — motivating *factorial* models in the next slides.

## Slide 33 — Factorial HMM
State at time t is represented by M variables, each evolving (almost) independently. Transitions are constrained: M separate K-by-K matrices instead of one `K^M`-by-`K^M` matrix. Much more parameter-efficient.

## Slide 34 — Tree-Structured HMM
Factorial HMM assumes factors are independent. Tree-structured HMM allows dependency by *ordering* the state variables, yielding a tree of conditional dependencies between the M state factors.

## Slide 35 — Switching State Space Model
A hybrid: factorial/tree HMMs use discrete state variables; state-space models use continuous M-dimensional real-valued vectors with linear dynamics. A *switching state space model* uses a discrete variable to select among (or "switch between") real-valued dynamics — effectively a mixture of linear experts.

## Slide 36 — Switching State Space Models (diagram)
Image-only slide that diagrams the switching SSM structure.

## Slide 37 — Approximate Inference and Intractability
For advanced models (factorial, tree, switching), exact inference becomes computationally intractable, motivating approximate inference — the topic of the next slides.

## Slide 38 — Approximation 1: Gibbs Sampling
Start with random hidden states; update each variable stochastically according to its conditional distribution given its *Markov blanket* (children, parents, parents of children). A variable is conditionally independent of the rest of the graph given its Markov blanket. The slide notes the specialization for factorial HMM.

## Slide 39 — Approximation 2: Variational Methods
A deterministic alternative: seek a tractable Q that maximizes the lower bound F on the log-likelihood, alternating updates of parameters and Q. Formula images render the variational bound.

## Slide 40 — Mean field for factorial HMM
The simplest variational choice: assume all state variables are independent conditioned on observations, so Q factorizes. For K-dim binary state vectors the parameters are the per-variable means; the Markov blanket is taken into account in the optimization.

## Slide 41 — Structured Approximation to Factorial
A more powerful variational family: variables independent *across chains* but with within-chain structure. Ignores common effects but keeps within-chain transitions and an additional "time-varying bias" term.

## Slide 42 — Bayesian HMM
The deck closes on overfitting: too many parameters relative to data cause poor test performance. Model selection (number of hidden states, arcs) is addressed by cross-validation, regularization (complexity penalties on the likelihood), or full Bayesian integration with priors that are updated by data.

## Deck-level takeaway

A 42-slide tour of probabilistic-sequence modeling that starts from joint factorization and graphical models, presents HMM as the canonical discrete-state instance, derives its learning machinery in full (forward-backward / Baum-Welch as belief propagation + EM, Viterbi as max-replacement), and then walks the reader through three escapes from the `K^M` state explosion: factorial / tree-structured HMM (parameter reduction via decomposition), switching state-space models (discrete + continuous hybrid), and approximate inference (Gibbs sampling, mean-field and structured variational). The pedagogical arc is "factor the joint → represent the factorization → infer on the representation → approximate when exact inference breaks".
