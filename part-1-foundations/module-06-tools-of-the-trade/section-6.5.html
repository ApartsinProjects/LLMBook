External Reading & Communities

Section 6.5

Part I is a foundation. Where you go next depends on whether you want to read the originals, follow the field, or get unstuck when an install fails at midnight. This section lists the resources that are still worth the click as of 2026, organized by purpose.

6.5.1 Textbooks worth owning

Three textbooks cover the same conceptual territory as Part I, in more depth and with more rigor than any blog post.

  • Jurafsky & Martin, Speech and Language Processing (3rd edition draft, regularly updated). The canonical NLP textbook. Chapters on n-gram models, neural LMs, attention, and the transformer are still the cleanest introductions available.
  • Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, Deep Learning (2016 but still useful as ML background). Read chapters 5 through 9 alongside Part I; skip the second half unless you are doing research.
  • Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning. The right reference when you need the statistics underpinnings, particularly Bayesian inference, regularization, and information theory.

6.5.2 Original papers to read at least once

The Transformer-era literature is small enough that you can read the foundational papers in a weekend. The ones below are non-optional.

  • Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" (2017). The Transformer.
  • Devlin et al., "BERT" (2018). Masked language modeling.
  • Radford et al., "Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training" (2018). GPT-1, the decoder-only argument.
  • Holtzman et al., "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" (2019). Nucleus sampling, the paper Chapter 5 builds on.
  • Kaplan et al., "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (2020). Read this and you will understand the second half of the book.

6.5.3 Tutorials, blogs, and lecture series

For the inevitable moments when a paper is unclear, these resources are reliable.

  • Jay Alammar, "The Illustrated Transformer". The pictures everyone has internalized. Pair with the paper.
  • Karpathy, "Neural Networks: Zero to Hero". Implements GPT and a tokenizer from scratch on video. The closest thing to having Karpathy as a TA.
  • Stanford CS224N (NLP with Deep Learning). Lecture videos and notes; the 2024 edition is the most recent at time of writing.
  • The Hugging Face NLP course. Free, practical, kept current.
  • Lilian Weng's blog. Long-form survey posts that double as paper indexes.

6.5.4 Communities

The field moves fast enough that conference proceedings lag the actual conversation. The places where the conversation happens:

  • X (formerly Twitter): still the fastest path to paper announcements and back-channel debate, despite its problems.
  • Bluesky: has absorbed a substantial fraction of academic ML in 2025-2026.
  • r/MachineLearning: the long-form discussion forum that survives every platform shift.
  • Hugging Face forums: best signal-to-noise for "why does this transformers code fail" questions.
  • EleutherAI Discord: invite-link-on-the-website, the closest the open-source LLM community has to a town square.

6.5.6 The freshness problem

The half-life of LLM tooling is short. The version of transformers that worked when you read this paragraph may be stale within a year. Appendix R tracks what changed in 2026 specifically, including breaking-API events, model release dates, and the URLs that have moved. When in doubt, trust the appendix over the chapter.

A working rule

For any given question, search arXiv first, the Hugging Face Hub second, and Stack Overflow last. Stack Overflow answers older than 18 months for transformers-related questions are usually wrong; arXiv is the source of truth and the Hub is the source of running code.
